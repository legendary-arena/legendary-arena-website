## Channel Positioning

The YouTube channel is the **transitional CTA** the homepage needs.
"Watch gameplay" links here. Every video reinforces the core positioning:
**every game is fair, every result is provable, every rank means
something.**

The channel serves all five Player Needs Pyramid levels and all three
content modes (Sales, Narrative, Authority). Four series divide the
work: three long-form series for conversion + one Shorts series for
discovery.

**Channel name rationale:** "Legendary Arena" — not "BarefootBetters."
Matches search intent, clean product association, no brand confusion,
better YouTube SEO. BarefootBetters is a separate business identity
with a different audience; if it gets a channel, it gets its own.

## SB7 Framework for Video

The homepage review template defines seven StoryBrand elements. Every
video must employ them — but the surface structure varies by series and
content mode. This section maps SB7 to the video medium so each episode
reinforces the full story arc, not just the piece its series owns.

### The Character and Their Desire

The viewer is the hero, not Legendary Arena. Every video must open a
**story gap** — the distance between where the viewer is and where they
want to be. When we name the desire, the viewer subconsciously asks
"Will I get it?" That question is what keeps them watching.

**Single customer desire (anchors the entire channel):**

> A card game that rewards your brain, not your wallet.

If a video doesn't connect to this desire — directly or by implication
— it doesn't belong on the channel.

### The Villain + Three Problem Levels

The anchoring villain is **the pay-to-win model** — the system that
sells power instead of rewarding skill. Every video should touch at
least one problem level, and the strongest episodes hit all three:

| Level | Definition | Video Application |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| **External** | The tangible frustration: "Top decks are locked behind money or grind." | Show the problem happening — concrete, visible, undeniable. |
| **Internal** | How it makes the viewer *feel*: "It feels like your skill doesn't matter." | Name the emotion. This is what drives engagement — people buy solutions to internal problems. |
| **Philosophical** | Why it's simply wrong: "Games should reward thinking — not spending." | Use "ought" and "should" language. This is the moral anchor. |

**Scripting rule:** When framing any problem in a video, do not stop at
the external level. The internal level is what creates emotional
engagement. The philosophical level is what earns shares.

### The Guide (Empathy + Authority)

The host is the guide — Yoda, not Luke. The guide has two qualities,
and both are required:

- **Empathy:** "We've played those games too. It's frustrating." — Prove
  you understand the viewer's world before offering solutions.
- **Authority:** Demonstrated competence — show the system working, cite
  verified results, display technical depth.

**Trust = Empathy + Authority.** A video that demonstrates authority
without empathy reads as a sales pitch. A video that shows empathy
without authority reads as commiseration.

| Series | Primary Guide Quality | How |
|--------|----------------------|-----|
| Building the Arena | Authority | Technical depth, live demos, system transparency |
| How to Play | Authority + Empathy | Competence (teaching) + understanding ("this part confused me too") |
| Across the Table | Empathy | Shared experience, genuine reactions, "we've been there" |

### The Plan

The plan removes confusion and fear. It appears in two forms:

- **Process plan (removes confusion):** Build your deck -> Play the
  scenario -> Earn your rank. The "How to Play" series *is* this plan
  in video form.
- **Agreement plan (removes fear):** The Fair Play Promise — no
  pay-to-win, no artificial time-gates, no disruptive balance patches.
  Reinforced in every "Building the Arena" episode.

### Call to Action (Direct + Transitional + Email)

Every video must include both types:

- **Direct CTA:** Challenge the viewer to act — "Play one fair game."
  Uses identity language, not generic access language.
- **Transitional CTA:** Lower-risk next step for viewers not ready to
  commit — "Watch the next video," "Join the Discord," "Subscribe."
  Stakes a claim to territory and creates reciprocity.
- **Email CTA (Newsletter):** The lowest-friction owned-channel
  conversion. "Get the free deck-building primer — link in description."
  Use the lead magnet to justify the ask. Email is the only audience
  asset YouTube cannot take away. Every viewer who subscribes to the
  list is worth more than a subscriber who never plays.

The direct CTA should appear at minimum in the hook and the outro.
Transitional CTAs appear mid-video when the content naturally earns
them. The email CTA appears in the outro and in the pinned comment
on every video.

**Lead magnet:** A short, immediately useful PDF or guide gated behind
email signup at `https://legendary-arena.com/get-started`. The value
proposition mirrors the channel's positioning — not generic game tips,
but something only Legendary Arena can offer:

> "The Deck-Builder's Primer: 12 Cards Most Players Build Wrong (and
> Why the Fair Game Changes Everything)"

Link in every video description as:
`https://legendary-arena.com/get-started?utm_source=youtube&utm_medium=video&utm_campaign=[slug]`

### Failure Stakes + Success Vision

Every story needs stakes — what happens if the viewer doesn't act? And
a success vision — what life looks like when they do.

**Failure stakes (use sparingly — salt, not the main course):**

> Stay in pay-to-win systems, and your skill will never matter.

**Success vision (resolve all three problem levels):**

- **External:** Every match is decided by your choices.
- **Internal:** You feel the satisfaction of real competition.
- **Philosophical:** You're part of a fair system.

These don't need to be stated explicitly in every video. But the
strongest episodes — especially "Building the Arena" #1 and "Across
the Table" guest episodes — should touch them.

### Identity Transformation

The deepest SB7 motivation: the viewer wants to *become someone
different*. The channel's job is to make this transformation visible.

| From | To |
|------|----|
| Grinding for cards | Competing with strategy |
| Frustrated player | Respected competitor |
| "The game is rigged" | "The game is fair" |

"Across the Table" is the primary vehicle for transformation — guests
arrive as one identity and the episode reveals another. But every
series contributes: "Building the Arena" transforms skeptics into
believers, "How to Play" transforms beginners into competent players.

### Copy Direction: Anchor to Internal Feelings

When scripting any video, reframe external/feature language into
internal/feeling language:

| Instead of (external/feature) | Say (internal/feeling) |
|------------------------------|----------------------|
| "Skill-first game" | "Finally, a card game you can trust" |
| "Deterministic engine" | "Nothing is hidden — you can prove it" |
| "PAR-based scoring" | "Your rank means something real" |
| "Content-as-data architecture" | "New content never breaks what you've learned" |
| "Replay verification" | "Every result is provable" |

This table is the single most important scripting tool in this
document. Feature language is for the dev team. Feeling language is
for the audience.

### Messaging Priority Rule (Content Sequencing)

The Player Needs Pyramid is strict — lower levels must be established
before higher levels land. This applies to video sequencing:

1. "Play instantly in your browser." (L1 — Access)
2. "Every game is verifiable. Nothing is hidden." (L2 — Trust)
3. "Join players who believe skill should decide." (L3 — Community)
4. "Your rank reflects how well you play." (L4 — Recognition)
5. "A training ground for mastery." (L5 — Mastery)

The content calendar follows this order. Week 1 establishes trust (L2).
Week 2 establishes access (L1). Weeks 3-4 begin building community
(L3). Recognition and mastery content comes later, after the foundation
is set.

If a video leads with Level 5 messaging ("become a master strategist")
before the channel has established Level 2 ("this game is fair"), the
higher-level promise has no foundation and won't land.

## Non-Negotiable Production Rules

These rules apply to **every video** across all series. They address
YouTube's distribution mechanics — the algorithm rewards retention,
curiosity, and Shorts volume. Strong content that ignores these
mechanics gets buried.

### Diagnose Before Demonstrating

Open every video by diagnosing the viewer's problem before demonstrating
the feature that solves it. The instinct is to pitch too early; the
channel's job is to make the viewer *feel* the problem first, then show
the bridge.

Do **not** open with:
- "Today I'm showing the replay system."
- "Here's how PAR scoring works."
- "We built a leaderboard."

Open with:
- "Ever wonder if a game result was actually fair?"
- "Most rankings tell you who played the *most*, not who played the *best*."
- "If nobody can verify the match, the rank doesn't mean anything."

Only after the viewer recognizes the problem does the video show the
Legendary Arena system that resolves it. This is the video-medium
expression of the homepage's non-negotiable problem-before-product
ordering rule.

### Objection-Driven Topics

A vein of high-intent topics comes straight from the
[Objection Library](homepage-appendix.md#objection-library): take an
objection a player raises before they'll commit and answer it as a
video. These pull in viewers already weighing the decision.

- Do digital card games always get the rules wrong?
- Can a leaderboard actually prove skill?
- Is pay-to-win avoidable in online card games?
- What changes when every result is replay-verifiable?
- Why most rankings reward grind instead of mastery

### Discovery Requirements

Every video must:

1. Use curiosity-driven title framing. Two proven formats:
   - **Curiosity gap:** "Can you actually PROVE...?" / "Why does every
     game...?" — the viewer must click to resolve the question.
   - **Result-first:** "I Built X in Y Time" / "I Tested X vs Y" /
     "I [did thing] — here's what happened." Show the outcome first,
     then rewind. Works especially well for dev log episodes where you
     already have the answer — lead with it.
   Both formats work. Curiosity gap attracts search traffic; result-
   first drives cold-audience feed impressions. Never use product-
   focused descriptions ("Our new feature does X").
2. Show outcome or tension within the first 15 seconds
3. Create a contradiction or question the viewer needs resolved
4. Be convertible into 3-7 Shorts clips

### Single Goal Per Video

Each video must declare **one primary conversion goal**:

| Primary Goal | When | CTA Emphasis |
|-------------|------|-------------|
| Play conversion | Tutorials, feature reveals | Direct CTA dominates |
| Email signup | Trust/fairness deep-dives, strategy content | Email CTA dominates |
| Subscriber growth | First videos, Shorts, guest episodes | Transitional CTA dominates |

All CTAs in the video must support the primary goal. Secondary CTAs
may appear but must not compete for attention. Multiple equal-weight
CTAs create decision fatigue and reduce action rates.

Declare the primary goal in the Series Assignment (Step 2 of the
production workflow) before scripting begins.

### Mid-Video Conversion Moment

Most viewers never reach the outro. Every video must include a
**mid-video CTA** placed at the moment of highest value delivery —
when the viewer has just seen the payoff and is most motivated to act.

Examples:
- "If this changes how you see rankings, play one game right now —
  link in the description."
- "This is the part that convinced me it was worth building. Try it
  yourself."
- "That's the fair play engine in action. See it for yourself."

The mid-video CTA must match the video's primary goal. Place it
immediately after the strongest demonstration, insight, or reaction
— not at an arbitrary midpoint.

### CTA Standard

CTAs must challenge identity or create curiosity. Avoid generic "try
it" language.

| Instead of | Use |
|------------|-----|
| "Try it yourself at play.legendary-arena.com" | "Test if your rank actually means something." |
| "Play now" | "Play one fair game — see if you notice the difference." |
| "Check it out" | "Prove your last game wasn't luck." |
| "Subscribe to our newsletter" | "Get the free deck-building primer — link in description." |
| "Sign up for updates" | "The primer covers the 12 cards most players build wrong — free, link below." |

### Retention Rules

Each video must include:

- **Hook (0-15 sec):** Show the outcome first, ask a question, or
  create tension. Example: "This game looks random... but I can prove
  it isn't." Use a proven structure from the Hook Library
  (`C:\pcloud\LA\video-assets\hooks\`).
- **First 30 seconds gate:** By 0:30, the video must have delivered a
  payoff, confirmed its promise, or escalated the tension. If
  retention drops below 50% at 30 seconds in the performance review,
  the hook is mandatory re-cut. YouTube heavily weights early retention.
- **Value density (every 15-30 sec):** Every 15-30 second window must
  deliver a new insight, show a new outcome, or escalate the idea. If
  a segment doesn't do any of these — cut it. Filler kills retention.
- **Pattern interrupts (every 60-90 sec):** Cut to a replay moment,
  insert a zoom/UI highlight, add a question overlay. Breaks the
  monotony signal that kills retention.
- **Mid-video CTA:** Placed at peak value delivery (see above).
- **End screen:** Point to the **next video in the series path**, not a
  random recommendation. "Watch this next — it shows exactly why that
  move worked" beats "Next episode..."

## Series 1: "Building the Arena" (Dev Log)

**Content Mode:** C (Authority)
**Pyramid Levels:** L2 (Trust) + L3 (Community)
**Cadence:** Biweekly or on release milestones

### What It Is

Progress updates, new feature reveals, and design decisions explained.
The host is the guide showing the work behind the product — not a hero
talking about himself.

**Build-first framing:** These episodes document systems that already
exist — write them after the feature ships, not before. Title them
accordingly: result-first formats ("I Built X" / "We Shipped X" / "I
Tested X vs Y") land harder than curiosity gaps here because you
already have the answer. Lead with it.

### Why It Works

This is the strongest authority signal before testimonials and traction
metrics exist. Transparency about *how* the game is built reinforces
the trust differentiator (Problem #4: hidden mechanics erode trust).
Showing the deterministic engine, the replay system, and the
content-as-data architecture *in action* is proof that the Fair Play
Promise is real.

### Episode Format (~8-15 min)

1. Hook: outcome or contradiction (15 sec)
2. What shipped since last time (30 sec recap)
3. One feature deep-dive — show it working, explain the *why* (the
   problem it solves from the catalog)
4. What's next (quick roadmap tease)
5. Identity CTA

### Starter Episodes

| # | Discovery Title | Problem Catalog | Hook (first 15 sec) |
|---|----------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Can you actually PROVE a game was fair? | #10, #5 | Show a replay hash verification, then rewind to explain |
| 2 | We added 40 card sets and changed zero lines of game code | #16 | Show the before/after card count, then explain content-as-data |
| 3 | We made pay-to-win literally impossible — here's how | #1 | Show the system that blocks purchasable power |
| 4 | Why most game rankings are fake (and how to fix them) | #7, #6 | Show a manipulated leaderboard vs. a PAR-verified one |
| 5 | This digital card game plays EXACTLY like the real one | #11, #12 | Side-by-side: physical card ruling vs. engine resolution |

### SB7 Alignment

Each episode follows Problem -> Deep Analysis -> Solution -> Result
(Mode C). The problem opens the video. The product resolves it. The
viewer sees it working.

**Guide positioning:** This series carries the heaviest authority load
on the channel. The host demonstrates competence through technical
depth — but every episode must include at least one empathy beat
("We've been burned by this too" / "This is the part that frustrated
us as players"). Authority without empathy reads as a tech demo, not
a story.

**Three-level scripting:** Each starter episode maps to at least one
problem from the catalog. When scripting, don't stop at the external
level ("leaderboards can be gamed"). Name the internal feeling ("your
rank doesn't mean anything if others can cheat") and the philosophical
anchor ("competitive integrity requires proof, not trust").

**Agreement plan reinforcement:** Every episode implicitly reinforces
the Fair Play Promise. When showing a feature, connect it back to one
of the three commitments: no pay-to-win, no time-gates, no disruptive
balance patches.

**Content Mode:** A (Sales) for introductory episodes, C (Authority) for advanced
**Pyramid Levels:** L1 (Access) + L5 (Mastery)
**Cadence:** Front-load 5-8 core episodes, then add as features ship

### What It Is

Walkthroughs of cards.legendary-arena.com (card browser, set
exploration, deck theory) and play.legendary-arena.com (match setup,
gameplay, replays, leaderboards).

### Why It Works

This is the process plan in video form — "Build your deck -> Play the
scenario -> Earn your rank." It removes confusion (L1) and gives
mastery-seekers (L5) a path to improvement. It also doubles as the
transitional CTA content: visitors who aren't ready to play can watch
someone else do it first.

### Episode Format (~5-10 min)

1. Hook: show the end result first (15 sec)
2. "Here's what we're doing today" (one clear goal)
3. Walkthrough with live UI
4. Tips / what to watch for
5. Identity CTA + specific next video link

### SB7 Alignment

Introductory episodes are Mode A — problem ("this looks complicated")
-> product ("here's how simple it actually is") -> result ("you just
played a full game"). Advanced episodes shift to Mode C with deeper
analysis.

**Guide positioning:** This series balances authority (teaching
competence) with empathy ("this part confused me the first time too").
The host is a guide who has walked the path, not an expert lecturing
from above.

**Process plan as narrative arc:** The tutorial track mirrors the
homepage process plan — Build your deck -> Play the scenario -> Earn
your rank. Each episode is a step in that plan. The viewer should feel
the confusion lifting as they progress through the playlist.

**Story gap per episode:** Every tutorial must open with what the
viewer can't do yet ("You've never seen the board before — it looks
like a lot") and close with what they now can ("You just read the
entire board state in three seconds"). That gap is what earns the
watch.

## Series 3: "Across the Table" (Interview + Gameplay)

**Content Mode:** B (Narrative)
**Pyramid Levels:** L3 (Community) + L4 (Recognition)
**Cadence:** See phased rollout below

### What It Is

The host sits down with a player, content creator, tabletop enthusiast,
or game designer. They play Legendary Arena together while they talk.
The game is the hot wings — it creates natural tension, pauses,
reactions, and shared moments. The conversation flows around the
gameplay, not in spite of it.

### Why It Works

This is the testimonials section, the community signal, and the identity
transformation narrative — all in one format. Every episode is a
before/after story told live. The guest's reactions to the fairness, the
replay verification, and the rules enforcement are organic proof that
lands harder than any homepage bullet point.

### The "Hot Ones" Parallel

Hot Ones works because the wings are a shared experience that strips
away the interview veneer. The game does the same — it creates genuine
reactions, strategic disagreements, and real-time proof of the product.

- **Escalation mechanic:** Start with a simple scenario, ramp to harder
  ones in later episodes. The game *is* the escalation.
- **Inherently watchable:** The format works even if you don't know the
  guest — the gameplay carries the entertainment value.
- **Organic product demonstration:** Every episode shows the product
  working without being a product pitch.

### Phased Rollout

This series has the highest production friction (guests, conversation
quality, scheduling). Phase it in rather than launching cold.

| Phase | Weeks | Approach |
|-------|-------|----------|
| Solo warmup | 1-4 | Solo gameplay + commentary. "Across the Table (Solo Mode)" — play as different archetypes: "Playing like a competitive grinder," "Playing like a casual first-timer." Builds the format, tests the rhythm, keeps cadence stable without guest dependency. |
| First guest | 5-8 | One guest episode. Low pressure — pick someone you already know. |
| Scale | 9+ | Monthly guest episodes, ramping to biweekly as the player base and guest pipeline grow. |

### Episode Format (~20-40 min)

1. **Hook:** Tease a moment from later in the game (15 sec)
2. **Intro the guest** — who they are, their relationship to card games
   or tabletop (30 sec)
3. **Set up the scenario together** — natural tutorial moment for new
   viewers
4. **Play + talk.** Let the game create the rhythm. Key conversation
   beats:
   - "What's your history with card games / Legendary?"
   - Natural reaction moments when the engine resolves something complex
   - "What surprised you about this?" (organic testimonial)
   - The guest's competitive identity — do they grind? Optimize? Play
     for fun?
5. **Post-game:** Look at the replay together, review the score, check
   PAR
6. **Identity CTA:** Guest's channel/project + challenge CTA

### Guest Targets (Priority Order)

| Priority | Guest Type | Why | Cross-Promo Value |
|----------|-----------|-----|-------------------|
| 1 | Active players | First testimonials — their stories are your proof | Low (small audience) but high authenticity |
| 2 | Tabletop content creators | Legendary / Marvel Champions / deck-builder YouTubers | High — natural audience overlap |
| 3 | Board game reviewers | They understand the physical-vs-digital tension (Problems #21-28) | High — review authority transfers |
| 4 | Game designers | They'll appreciate the deterministic engine and rules fidelity | Medium — niche but high authority |
| 5 | Competitive card game players | MTG, Pokemon TCG players — the pay-to-win villain resonates hardest here | High — large audiences, strong emotional hook |

### SB7 Alignment

Mode B — the problem is woven into conversation ("I'm so tired of
pay-to-win"), the product appears as context (the game they're playing),
and the result is felt, not declared (the guest's genuine reactions).
This is the most powerful content mode for trust-building because
nothing is scripted.

**Guide positioning:** This series carries the heaviest empathy load on
the channel. The host and guest share a common frustration — the
villain is something they've both experienced. Authority emerges
organically from the game itself (the engine resolving a complex
interaction correctly, the replay proving a result, PAR scoring a
performance).

**Identity transformation in real time:** This series is the primary
vehicle for the From/To arc. The guest arrives as a "frustrated player"
or a "skeptic" and the episode reveals them becoming a "respected
competitor" or a "believer." Don't force it — the game creates the
transformation naturally. But be aware of it in editing: the moments
where a guest's posture shifts are the most valuable footage in the
episode.

**Failure stakes (implicit):** When guests talk about their history
with other games — the grind, the pay-to-win, the hidden mechanics —
they're narrating the failure stakes without being prompted. These
moments are the "what happens if you don't act" section of the
homepage, delivered as authentic testimony.

**Three-level problem surfacing:** The best "Across the Table"
conversations will naturally hit all three problem levels. Guide the
conversation toward:
- External: "What's the worst experience you've had with [pay-to-win /
  hidden mechanics / rules drift]?"
- Internal: "How did that make you feel about the game?"
- Philosophical: "Do you think games should work that way?"

The guest's own words become the most persuasive copy on the channel.

**Physical vs. digital tension:** For guests who play the physical card
game, the Scalability theme (Problems #21-28) is a natural
conversation thread. Position digital not as "better" but as
"everything you love about the game — without the friction that stops
you from playing." This respects the guest's identity as a tabletop
player (L2 trust requirement) while opening the story gap.

## Series 4: "Arena Clips" (Shorts)

**Content Mode:** All three (clips inherit the mode of their source)
**Pyramid Levels:** All (discovery layer — casts the widest net)
**Cadence:** 3-5 per week, cut from existing long-form videos

### What It Is

The discovery engine. YouTube Shorts are how new audiences find the
channel. Long-form converts; Shorts discover. Without this series, the
channel relies entirely on external traffic (homepage, social) for
growth — that's a ceiling, not a strategy.

### Why It Works

Shorts feed the algorithm's appetite for high-frequency, high-retention
content. They cost almost nothing to produce (cut from existing footage)
and each one is a free impression lottery ticket pointing back to the
full video.

### Clip Sources

| Source Series | Clip Types |
|--------------|------------|
| Building the Arena | Feature micro-demos (10-20 sec), "wait, it can do THAT?" moments |
| How to Play | Quick tips, before/after replay comparisons, "one thing most players miss" |
| Across the Table | Reaction moments, guest surprises, gameplay turning points |

### Enforcement Rule

Every long-form video must produce **minimum 3, maximum 7 Shorts
clips.** This is not optional — it is the top-of-funnel fuel that makes
the rest of the system work.

### Shorts Format

1. Hook in first 2 seconds (text overlay + visual)
2. One idea, one payoff
3. End with channel name + "Full video in comments"

### SB7 Alignment

Shorts are the story gap in its purest form — open a question in 2
seconds, resolve it in 30, leave the viewer wanting the full version.
Each Short should touch exactly one problem level:

- **External Shorts:** Show the problem happening. "This is what
  pay-to-win looks like." / "Watch the engine resolve this instantly."
- **Internal Shorts:** Name the feeling. "That moment when you realize
  your rank is actually real." / Guest reaction clips.
- **Philosophical Shorts:** State the principle. "Games should reward
  thinking, not spending." / "Competition without verification isn't
  real competition."

Shorts are the top-of-funnel villain introduction. Many viewers will
meet the pay-to-win villain for the first time in a Short. Make it
land in under 5 seconds.

### Shorts as Testing Engine

Shorts produce feedback 3x faster than long-form. Use them to test
before committing to a full video:

| What to Test | How | Signal |
|-------------|-----|--------|
| Hook wording | Post the same clip with 2 different hooks as separate Shorts | Higher-retention version wins |
| Topic angle | Post a Short exploring a topic before scripting the full video | Views + comments signal demand |
| Title framing | Use the Short's title as a dry run for the long-form title | Impressions + CTR |

**Rule:** Top-performing Shorts (top 20% by views) become candidates
for full-length episodes. If a Short outperforms expectations, fast-
track its topic to the content calendar. If a topic Short gets zero
traction, reconsider whether the long-form video is worth producing.

### Thumbnails / Branding

Each series gets a distinct visual template so subscribers recognize
what they're getting at a glance:

| Series | Visual Style |
|--------|-------------|
| Building the Arena | Dev/code aesthetic — dark background, feature screenshot, progress indicator |
| How to Play | Clean UI screenshots — bright, instructional, step-number overlay |
| Across the Table | Two people + the game board — conversational, warm lighting |

### Cross-Linking

- Every "Building the Arena" episode links to the relevant "How to
  Play" tutorial
- Every "Across the Table" episode links to the "How to Play" starter
  episode
- Homepage gets a "Watch gameplay" transitional CTA linking to the
  channel or a curated playlist
- Each video description includes links to play.legendary-arena.com,
  cards.legendary-arena.com, the "Start Here" playlist, and the
  newsletter/lead magnet signup — all with UTM parameters
- Each Short links to its source long-form video

### Community Posts

Unlocked at 500 subscribers. Free top-of-funnel impressions that cost
almost nothing to produce. Post 2-3 times per week once available.

**Content types:**

| Post Type | Frequency | Purpose |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Poll: "What frustrates you most about card games?" | Weekly | Surfaces villain language from your own audience; use responses in future scripts |
| Behind-the-scenes screenshot from Step 3 research | Weekly | Builds parasocial guide identity before the video ships |
| "Early look" — one screenshot or 15-sec clip from upcoming video | Per video | Seeds curiosity before publish day |
| Newsletter teaser — one tip from the lead magnet | Monthly | Drives email signups from existing subscribers |
| Question card: "What do you want us to cover next?" | Monthly | Free content calendar research |

**Rule:** Community Posts are not production videos — they cost 5
minutes, not 5 hours. A blurry behind-the-scenes photo is fine. The
goal is presence and reciprocity, not polish.

### Playlists as Binge Paths

Playlists are not filing cabinets — they are **viewer journeys**. Each
playlist must have an entry video, a progression sequence, and an end
state outcome. The end screen of every video points to the next video
in the same binge path, not a random recommendation. This extends
session watch time, which YouTube rewards with more impressions.

| Playlist | Entry Video | Journey | End State |
|----------|-------------|---------|-----------|
| "Start Here" | BtA #1 (villain intro) | BtA #1 → HtP #1 → HtP #2 → HtP #3 | Viewer has played their first game |
| "How to Play" | HtP #1 (first game) | HtP #1 → #2 → #3 → #4 → #5 | Viewer understands scoring + replays |
| "Building the Arena" | BtA #1 (fairness proof) | Chronological dev log | Viewer trusts the system |
| "Across the Table" | First guest episode | Guest episodes in order | Viewer sees real player transformations |
| "Why It's Fair" | BtA #3 (pay-to-win episode) | Curated trust-focused mix from all series | Viewer is convinced fairness is real |

**Session watch rule:** Every end screen must link to the next video
in the same playlist path. If the video is the last in a path, link
to the entry video of the next logical path (e.g., end of "How to Play"
→ first "Across the Table" episode).

### Email Funnel Segmentation (Phase 2)

Launch with the single lead magnet (Deck-Builder's Primer). Once the
channel has 20+ videos and email signup data, segment by content theme:

| Content Theme | Lead Magnet | Target Audience |
|--------------|-------------|----------------|
| How to Play | Beginner's Quick-Start Guide | New players, tutorial viewers |
| Fairness / Trust | The Fair Game Report: How We Eliminated Pay-to-Win | Skeptics, competitive players |
| Strategy / Mastery | Advanced Deck Optimization Guide | Returning players, leaderboard chasers |

Each video's email CTA points to the lead magnet that matches its
theme. This increases conversion because the offer matches the
viewer's current interest.

**Phase 1 (now):** Single lead magnet, single signup link. Track which
video slugs drive the most signups via UTM.
**Phase 2 (20+ videos):** Create theme-specific lead magnets. Route
each video's email CTA to the matching magnet.

**What this channel does *not* own:** the post-signup nurture sequence
(welcome email, ongoing follow-up) lives in the email pipeline, not here.
The welcome automation is specced in
[WP-018](../ai/work-packets/WP-018-brevo-automation.md), and the funnel
stages and subscriber-state model in `docs/email-automation.md`.
Automated multi-step drips are deliberately out of scope in WP-018 and
require their own governing WP — so the channel's job is to feed
qualified signups into that pipeline with clean UTM attribution, not to
define drip content. A "value → objection → breakup" follow-up arc is a
sound design, but it belongs in that future drip WP, mapped to the
subscriber-state model (Pending → Confirmed → Welcomed → Active), not
bolted onto the channel plan.

### Top Video Reinforcement (Monthly)

Each video is not a one-shot. Monthly, identify the top 20% performing
videos and double down:

1. **Create a follow-up video** — go deeper on the topic that resonated
2. **Cut 3-5 new Shorts** — re-edit the best moments with new hooks
3. **Test new title/thumbnail variants** — A/B test in YouTube Studio

Doubling down on winners is the highest-ROI growth move on YouTube.
Most creators treat every video as independent — you should treat top
performers as franchises.

### Weekly Production Cadence

Reference schedule for a one-video-per-week pace. Adjust days to fit
your actual schedule — the rhythm matters more than the specific days.

| Day | Focus |
|-----|-------|
| Monday | Pick topic; build or verify the demo works |
| Tuesday | Finish build; write script (or light script) |
| Wednesday | Record |
| Thursday | Step 6 (FFmpeg assembly) + Step 7 (Premiere edit) |
| Friday | Upload + schedule long-form; post Short 1 |
| Saturday | Shorts 2-3 on YouTube; cross-post prior Shorts to TikTok/Reels |
| Sunday | Check previous video metrics; research topic for next week |

**Build first, record second.** If the demo isn't ready by Tuesday,
slide the recording to the following week. A polished video that clears
the Good Enough Publish Rule ships; a rushed one drags down avg view
duration and poisons the algorithm signal.

get views.

## Success Criteria

The YouTube system is working when these trends are improving over a
rolling 30-day window. Absolute numbers don't matter at launch — trend
direction does.

| Signal | What It Proves | Healthy Trend |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| CTR improving over baseline | Packaging (title + thumbnail) is getting stronger | Week-over-week CTR increase on new videos |
| Avg view duration increasing | Content quality and retention engineering are working | Each month's avg duration > previous month |
| Shorts views increasing | Discovery layer is feeding the funnel | Shorts impressions growing faster than long-form |
| Email signups increasing | Owned-channel conversion is working | Signups per video trending up (not just total) |
| Play conversions increasing | The actual business goal is being served | UTM clicks -> first game events trending up |
| Production time per video decreasing | Pipeline automation and muscle memory are paying off | Time budget targets met without quality regression |
| Session watch time increasing | Binge paths are working — viewers watch 2+ videos | Avg session duration growing |

**When to worry:**
- CTR declining across 3+ consecutive videos → packaging problem
  (titles, thumbnails, or topic selection)
- Retention declining while CTR is stable → content problem (hooks,
  pacing, value density)
- Views growing but conversions flat → funnel problem (CTAs, landing
  page, or onboarding friction)
- Shorts views growing but long-form flat → Shorts aren't linking
  back effectively

**When to celebrate:** All four signals (CTR, retention, email, play
conversions) trending up simultaneously. That means the full system —
discovery, content, conversion, and funnel — is working together.

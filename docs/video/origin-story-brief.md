# Legendary Arena — Origin Story & Promo Film Brief

**Status:** thinking complete, nothing drafted.
**Purpose of this file:** carry the full creative brief into the repo so Claude Code (or a writer, or a director) can work from it without rebuilding the thinking.

Two deliverables come out of this material, in this order:

1. **The manuscript** — a prose origin story, ~2–3k words, single-sitting read, published on the Legendary Arena site. Explicitly *not* a book. A graphic-novel version is parked so it doesn't eat focus.
2. **The film** — a promo video adapted *from* the manuscript. Shoot long (~2 min) as the version people choose to watch, then cut 90s and 30s for paid placement.

---

## 1. The core idea

A story arc, not a product demo. A man alone and disconnected at the start; connected by the end. The game is the vehicle and is never explained.

**Underlying message:** having something in common produces connection.

**The feeling to leave the viewer with: "I can become."** Not relief, not sympathy. And in both directions — I could be the one taken in, or the one who takes someone in.

**Mission statement, to be handed to every department:**

> Someone saw who he could be before he could see it himself. He just had the courage to walk through the door.

The two halves are the **offer** and the **acceptance**. Both must be on screen.

---

## 2. The thesis

The real subject is **identity, not divorce**.

Jeff built himself out of roles — husband, father, engineer. Those roles were given, and every given thing can be withdrawn. Cindy ends the husband. The children grow up and thin out the father. Retirement ends the engineer. He looks underneath and finds nothing there.

The roles were **fish handed to him**. The year at the table is **learning to fish**.

> He doesn't end up happy because he has friends. He has friends because he became someone who can be one.

### Be, then have

The cleanest statement of the whole thing, and the candidate epigraph:

> God doesn't want us to have something. He wants us to be something.
> Satan doesn't want us to be something. He wants us to have something.

Order matters. Nourish the tree, then have the fruit. Be a fisherman, then have the fish. Jeff's error before the year is doing it backwards.

Every counterfeit in the story is an offer of **having**: the father's wealth, Egypt's food, the kingdoms, the fruit without the tree. Being is the only thing that can't be handed over.

### Why having never satisfies

It has to be defended. Even with plenty of it you live in fear, because you don't know how to replenish it — watching the balance shrink, afraid someone will take it. Being needs no defending.

**Take a fisherman's boat and he builds another. Take a rich man's money and he's finished.**

Which gives the real ending: Jeff isn't secure because he has friends. He's secure because he's become someone who can make them. **Lose the table and he'd build another one.**

### Widen the door

Deliberately don't over-index on the divorce. Retirement, graduation, a job change, children leaving home are the same mechanism — a role ends and you find out what was underneath. Readers who've never been divorced should still read it about themselves.

## 3. The manuscript

### Why it has to exist

Hugo **skips the becoming**. Candlesticks, then years later Valjean is a mayor with a factory. The in-between chapter is missing.

Scripture does the same. Enoch's city: *"in process of time"* — generations of becoming compressed into five words. Elijah didn't end as the prophet he started as; the chariot is the last line, not the story.

**The manuscript is deliberately that missing chapter.** The process of time, written out. Redemption in progress, unwitnessed. The reader knows what Jeff doesn't.

Supporting image: the practice is invisible to everyone who hears you play. Nobody sees the thousands of mistakes, just someone who can play. This is the only place the practice gets shown.

### Narrator

**Jeff looking back.** Gratitude is retrospective — you cannot feel it during. In the middle there is bitterness, or just grinding.

So the narrator is warm about a year that wasn't warm to live through, and the reader gets both at once. That's what stops it being bleak. Jeff telling it knows how it turned out; Jeff living it doesn't — and if he'd known what was coming, it wouldn't have been faith.

### Tone

Distinguished from Les Misérables: Hugo's is grace and redemption through Christ. **Jeff's is faith moving forward when none of it makes sense** — continuing without knowing the end result.

### The four states (the spine underneath the five movements)

| | |
|---|---|
| 1 | With someone, and alone — the marriage |
| 2 | Alone, and alone — the bottom |
| 3 | Alone, and all right — he becomes okay by himself |
| 4 | Happy with himself **and** with other people |

**The order is the point.** He gets okay alone *before* anyone arrives. This kills any reading of the ending as a rescue, or of people as compensation.

Model: the prodigal son. He was in the family and not happy in it, left, and had to become someone before he could go home. The father never changed and home never changed — what changed is that the son could finally be in it. The table was always going to have Jeff. He had to become someone who could sit at it.

### Five movements

**One — alone while married.**
Not a marriage ending. A man alone inside one. Jeff and Cindy physically together, connection gone, not fighting — two people in the same room who've stopped meeting there. He's still trying to reach her when she's already gone, which makes the later loneliness less of a change than he expects.

Matches the film's first frame exactly: Cindy with earbuds and phone glow; his hand entering the shot as he reaches over to speak.

**Two — the bottom.**
Pension but no house. Nowhere to live. Nobody is cruel to him — that's the point. Polite, busy, useful. Things still in bags in the spare room. Weeks passing while he calls it adjusting.

**Three — the doorway.**
Tex asks nothing and offers no advice. Just: there's a game Thursday. Jeff's half is picking up his keys — the smallest possible next step. First night he barely speaks. Doesn't matter. He came.

**Four — the year.**
The long one; it earns the ending. Thursdays stacking up. Bad weeks he nearly misses. Cindy's name on the phone, turned face-down. The pub he drives past. The man who never moves. Nobody teaches him anything and he changes without noticing, which is the only way it happens.

Persistence here isn't heroic in the moment — it's showing up again after a bad week. **"He kept turning up, badly, for a year."** The bad weeks have to be on the page; a clean run of Thursdays is a montage, not a story.

**Five — what it became.**
Years on. Grandchildren playing. The arena built. The diorama. And a new man at the table who barely speaks — dealt in without comment. The seat he now keeps for someone else.

### Seed material

The existing "it all started with a game" passage (Walt Disney's "it all started with a mouse"; Tex opening his home during the divorce; *"come sit at my table, you're not alone"*; game nights as the constructive space where men work through things shoulder to shoulder).

## 4. The middle: exit, stall, or step

The same choice, repeated every week. Three categories, not two.

**Exits.** The obvious ones are the pub and going back to Cindy — the second being an exit that isn't obviously self-destructive but is still escaping backwards. The sly ones matter more: working late so there's no evening to face, being the helpful one so nobody asks how you are, scrolling till two in the morning, deciding you're fine. **None of them look like quitting.**

**Stalling** is the most respectable-looking of the three and the hardest to spot. Waiting for conditions you've decided are required. The excuse just protects it — and a *true* excuse works better than a made-up one, because you can't argue with it.

- The Jaredites, four years on the beach. Knowing they have to cross, doing busy work near the water, waiting for it to happen. The Lord's first words are *why haven't you prayed*. The barges only get built once the waiting stops.
- The man at Bethesda. Thirty-eight years beside water that only healed whoever got in first. Asked *wilt thou be made whole*, he doesn't answer — he explains why it hasn't worked. **And he's right. Nobody did put him in.**

> **Being right is not the same as being free.**

**The next step** is almost always smaller than you'd script. Putting your keys in your pocket. Answering a text two days late instead of never.

Against stalling: **ask, seek, knock** — all verbs, and they escalate. Ask is a word. Seek costs time. Knock puts you at someone's door. Not *wait, rest, be still*.

In the film, Tex knocks first — grace runs ahead of the asking. But Jeff still has to open the door. That half is his.

### The excuse test

**"Both true, both useless."** Apply to every character.

The Bethesda man really did have nobody. The prodigal really did hit a famine — but he'd already wasted it on riotous living, and the famine just arrived in time to be blamed. A real event doing the work of a reason is the sharpest kind of excuse, because it isn't a lie.

Blame, bitterness, and the rest are the same move in different clothes: the problem is out there, so nothing in here has to change. Comfortable and completely stuck.

### Accountability

The prodigal's prepared speech is genuinely accountable — *I sinned, I'm no longer worthy to be called your son* — not "things went badly". The father interrupts it, but the son still had to mean it.

**You can't be restored to something you won't admit you left.**

Jeff's version: owning his half of thirty years while she's the one who left. That isn't fair. It's just the only move that gets him anywhere. Blame would have felt better and cost him the year.

### Victim mentality

The thing that blocks transformation. If Valjean adopts it, the candlesticks become a grievance and he stays a thief. The Thénardiers are Hugo's control group — same poverty, same hard world, no transformation, and their life gets worse.

**The difference isn't circumstance. It's what you decide you are.**

This makes the "nobody is a victim" tone guardrail load-bearing rather than a matter of taste: if Jeff is a victim of the divorce, no transformation is available to him.

### Courage

Separate from seeing the point of it. Change means being bad at something in front of people. Jeff at the first game night knows nothing, says nothing, stays anyway. **That's the real gate — not understanding.**

Going back is harder than leaving. The prodigal walks home rehearsing, expecting ridicule — and the older brother says exactly what he feared. The father runs to him, undignified for an old man, and takes the shame off before the speech is finished. But the son had to walk that road not knowing it would land that way.

## 5. Characters

### Jeff

Arc covered above. Crew-facing note: his true identity is the core of movement four. Husband, father, engineer — all withdrawable. What can't be taken is that he's a child of God. It was true in the spare room when he felt like nothing; he didn't earn it and he can't lose it. It's the *be*, not the *have*.

**Creation is the proof of it.** Making things is the family trade, and the tell is what the thing does: Legendary Arena puts people in a room together. The counterfeit always isolates you while promising the world. So the arena isn't just what Jeff did afterwards — it's evidence of what he became. A fisherman feeding people who are hungry.

### Tex Dealer — the mentor

Yoda, Obi-Wan, the bishop Myriel, the dad in the VW driveway. He never teaches or advises. **The mentoring is entirely in showing up, and his method is patience.** The seasons aren't decoration — they're the evidence he kept coming back.

His own arc, which he needs one of:

- A mentor needs a student. He has the skills, like a piano teacher with nobody to teach. **The mentor needs the student as much as the reverse** — a tree with nobody to eat the fruit hasn't finished the job. His joy is completion, not charity; he becomes fully himself when Jeff sits down.
- The bishop never sees Valjean again. Tex does better: he gets to watch it work.
- **He offers with no guarantee.** The bishop might have been robbed twice. Jeff might have drunk his way through that year. The offer has to be free or it isn't an offer — which makes the invitation braver than it looks on screen. Worth the actor knowing.

Elijah and Elisha as the model for the handover. Elisha asks for a double portion — more than his teacher had — which fits Jeff not ending as a copy of Tex but building the arena, something Tex never did. And Elijah tries three times to leave Elisha behind; he refuses to stay put. **The student has to insist.**

Family: wife Tanya, sons Caleb and Luke. The Dealers are the "Weasleys" who take him in. (Modelled on Tex's real family — model, not casting.)

### Cindy — the alternative, not a subplot

**She has the same crisis as Jeff.** That's what makes her structurally necessary. Her identity was mother; the children left; with the role gone she found nothing underneath. Empty nest, loss of focus, *"now it's my turn"* after years of sacrificing — so she went looking for external validation.

**Same wound, two answers: he waits in the dark, gets knocked on, and builds. She goes looking to be filled.**

- Her hesitation isn't a moment at the door — it's years. She stayed a long time with no connection, **waiting for it to arrive rather than making any** (the same error as Jeff — both waiting to be met). That's why she doesn't wobble when she finally goes.
- **She does find it, for a while.** Genuine happiness with someone else. She wasn't lying. It simply has no roots and runs out — and by then the thirty years are gone. Not punished.
- The tragedy is that she then stays. Going back feels impossible after what she said and did. She knows, and carries on. Still in the cage, no progress. **Not Master of the House — the Thénardiers are content in it. Closer to Javert without the bridge:** the realisation arrives and the door is shut behind her.

**Her mechanism is impatience.** Chop the tree down to get the fruit now. Kill the goose for all the golden eggs at once. Ask for the inheritance early — wanting the father dead so he can have it now. Everyone in this story who fails wants the harvest without the season. Roots thirty years deep don't come back by planting again in your fifties.

**The belief/behaviour mechanism.** When the two don't match you can change one or the other, and living in between is miserable. Changing the behaviour is repentance. Changing the belief is easier and nobody notices — you gradually stop believing the thing you're not doing. That explains her leaving the church without needing a scene: the belief had to go because the behaviour wasn't going back.

**What she loses is three things, not two:** the tree, the eggs, and the story she told herself about why it was worth doing. She expected to be seen as brave; instead people are quietly baffled, and she's left maintaining a version of events only she believes.

**She's hard to blame.** Nobody is wired for twenty-year returns; present things beat future things. Which makes Jeff the exception — and only because someone kept turning up while he couldn't see ahead either. **He didn't have twenty-year vision. He had Thursdays.**

**The structural difference:** he ends up with people, she ends up with a person. He built a table; she found a replacement. A table survives someone leaving it. One relationship carrying everything is fragile. And it is a moral difference, not neutral arithmetic — a gamble against known odds. Certainty now, on credit.

Leave her ending open. The reader is never told what happens to her. Mostly off the page — a hint, not a scene.

### The Bethesda man — contrast character

One bloke at the table. Same seat every week, tells the same story about his ex, hasn't moved. No judgement on him, just present. Costs almost nothing and makes Jeff's arc read differently, because he's sitting next to someone who didn't move.

### Cleanest statement of the structure

> Jeff and Cindy suffer the same pain. So do the father and the prodigal son. The separation. Neither chose it. The story is what each of them does with it.

The divorce isn't the subject. It's the identical starting line they walk away from in opposite directions.

## 6. Thematic reference library

Material to draw on when drafting. Not all of it needs to surface.

**Jacob 5, the olive tree.** Refusing the slow work of roots reaching down and branches reaching up; taking someone else's fruit because it's available now. The unglamorous year at the table *is* the roots.

**Alma 32, the seed.** You don't water it and get apples the next day, and if you neglect it the quality of the seed doesn't matter. But you know it's good long before there's fruit — the swelling and the sprouting. That's Jeff's small weekly reward: proof the thing is alive, not the harvest.

**Egypt.** The Israelites wanting to go back — not because it was good, but because it was known. The counterfeit is always backwards and always someone else's.

**The Jaredites.** They asked to be led to the best land and still had to make the journey, because the journey is what made them people who could live there. Israel wandered forty years and the ones who couldn't let go of Egypt didn't make it. **The promised land isn't handed over; you become someone who can hold it.**

**The woman with the issue of blood.** Twelve years is what made her push through a crowd she wasn't allowed to be in. One day in, she'd have waited and seen. The suffering produced the faith that reached out.

**Valjean's nineteen years.** What makes the candlesticks mean anything. A day in, the silver is just silver.

> **Editor's note: the length IS the meaning.** If Tex turns up in week two it's a kind gesture. A year in, it's the thing that saves him. The edit will be tempted to trim exactly that middle. Don't.

**Gratitude is retrospective.** Childbirth is the cleanest case — nobody is grateful mid-labour, nobody doubts it afterwards. The harder and more honest case is the wayward child, where the ending isn't good and gratitude becomes a choice: you have to go looking for the sweet parts. Nearer to Jeff and Cindy.

Where it lands: Jeff looking back calls the divorce the greatest blessing, because it was the transition point where he chose not to exit. The worst thing upstream of the best thing — nineteen years lead to the bishop, the bishop leads to Cosette. When he remarries he can tell his wife he'd never have met her otherwise.

**Piano practice.** You can't be given a skill. And nobody says *"once I'm a good piano player I'll start practising"*, because it's absurd — but *"once I feel better I'll go out"* is the same sentence and it passes. **Manuscript line: Jeff waiting until he feels like someone who goes to a card game.**

**Momentum.** It only exists once something is already moving. You can't build it from the bench. Strength arrives mid-motion, never before.

**Happiness** isn't a target — it's the by-product of living well and doing the thing properly. What falls off the branch of a life worked at.

**The candlesticks.** The bishop gives Valjean *objects*, not a speech — something he can hold and carry, kept for life as physical proof someone decided he was good. The hero cards do the same job for Jeff, which makes them worth keeping, not just playing. But the card is never the point. It's the excuse to be in the room.

**A stock of heroes.** When you're down you loop on your own circumstances. A stock of worthwhile stories breaks the loop — Valjean was in a worse spot, Captain America was a weakling. Not advice. A door. Tex's table hands Jeff that weekly without a word.

**The Mastermind's best attack** isn't arguing — it's rewriting the hero so there's nothing left to aim at. Retellings that make the hero secretly a fraud remove the model rather than refute it, and flatter the audience while doing it. Hence keeping the originals, the histories and the scriptures intact.

**Twelve-step parallels.** Step one — admitting powerlessness — sounds like the opposite of accountability but isn't: it's refusing the excuse that you've got it handled. Step four, the searching moral inventory. Steps eight and nine, amends. And the sponsor is a Tex: someone who's been through it, turns up, doesn't lecture — and is told the helping keeps *them* sober. Same as the tree needing someone to eat the fruit.

**The Secret Life of Walter Mitty.** Same order as Jeff's arc, lighter reference. Mitty's profile is empty because there's no life in it; he goes and builds one and she follows. He doesn't win her by becoming impressive.

**Despicable Me** — structural model. The surface is the caper; the real story underneath is a man becoming a father. Same relationship between the game on the surface and connection underneath.

**Transformation isn't complete at the end of part three.** It's complete when he gives it away in part two. Having a thing is an event; being the person who can get it is who you are — and that's what you can hand a friend going through the same thing. *"I know, because I passed through it."*

## 7. The film

### Series structure

Three films, one friendship (Jeff and Tex) across years:

1. **He's taken in.**
2. **He takes someone in** — his grandchildren bring a neighbourhood kid who has nowhere to be. Framed on the candlesticks: he was given a seat he didn't earn and passes it on.
3. **His health fails** — the shakes, can't shuffle or hold physical cards, so the friends move the game online and he's still at the table. Final image: five people in five rooms, one game, laughing at the same moment. (Senior-home setting suggested as a strengthener.)

**Part three is being made first**, rewritten as the full arc: disconnected marriage → divorce → long loneliness → the same room full of friends at the end, bookending the opening shot.

Parts one and two are still only sketched and need bringing up to part three's level.

### Craft decisions

- **Face hidden throughout** — POV or from behind — so the final smile is the first reveal. Alternate ending if the smile doesn't land in the rushes: reverse the shot, camera arcing behind him, the friends' lit faces visible and him a silhouette.
- **Reframe:** the man isn't the main character. The friendship is. He's the circumstances it happens to.
- **Opening frame:** start on Cindy — earbuds, phone glow, absorbed — hold ~2s, then his hand enters frame as he reaches to speak. Open mid-gesture, not on stillness.
- **Light:** dark at the start → daylight and a visible window with trees by the end.
- **Seasons** via holiday decorations (July 4th → autumn/Thanksgiving → Christmas → Easter/spring), shot Notting Hill–style: same table, same angle, redressed across one day.
- **Music:** Donny Osmond, "Starting All Over Again". Solo/instrumental at the start → full harmony and backing vocals at the end. Opens with a single warm chord lifted from the song's ending, played once, then silence; the same chord returns in full at the end.
- **Sound design:** pull all audio for one beat before the final table, then the full arrangement as the Fantastic Four card lands. (Modelled on the VW ad dropping its score.)
- **Hero cards mirror the arc:** Hulk early (anger, isolation), Daredevil or Captain America mid (beaten down, getting up), Fantastic Four at the end (a family). Tex's card is Bucky.
- **Tagline:** "Starting all over again." No pitch until a short end card — name and website, no price.
- **Dialogue:** little to none. Carried by camera angles, body language and music.
- **Locations:** two houses (marital home; Tex's, covering both spare room and dining-room game nights) plus an exterior grab shot. He loads a **pickup truck**, not a hired van.
- **Casting brief:** two men late fifties — one who can carry scenes from behind with no dialogue, one whose warmth reads instantly; a woman in her fifties who plays withdrawal without coldness; three or four twenty-somethings who read as an actual family.
- **Friendship models:** Butch and Sundance (affection as mockery, never sincerity), Danny Ocean and Rusty (whole exchanges in a glance), Helaman and Antipus (two friends holding a line unseen by each other, neither keeping score).

### Fixes already made

- **Cindy disappearing after the opening** → mid-film at Tex's table her name appears on his ringing phone and he turns it face-down, rhyming with her face-down phone in the opening. Her reason for calling is deliberately never answered.
- **Thin middle** → private grief where nobody sees it: alone in the truck at night, tissues, wiping his eyes, then parking and walking in as if fine. Later, Caleb and Luke turn up on his doorstep with the game.
- **Game decorative** → hero cards as mirrors; his arc is identity.
- **Redundant rescue** → both kept but differentiated. Tex's invitation early is a friend doing his duty; Caleb and Luke arriving unasked months later is a family claiming him (long version only).
- **Phone motif** trimmed to two beats. The invitation now arrives in person — **Tex in a lit doorway** while Jeff is in the dark spare room.

### Tone guardrails ("what it's not")

Nobody is the villain. Nobody is a victim. Not chasing a cultural moment. Not reinventing the brand for a new audience. Not asking the viewer to take a side — the friendship is simply a friendship.

Casting guardrail: everyone at the table must have a story reason to be there. Decide who each person is to him before casting, so casting serves the story and a good performance is a bonus rather than a rescue.

Accepts the piece may be called sentimental. Sentiment is the point.

### The veteran player

Resolves to one test and one guardrail. A long-time Legendary player wants the game shown in a light that gives them **status** — a non-player watching should think *"that looks like something I'd want to be part of."*

> **Never let the game look like a consolation prize.**

Beer-ad logic — the product borrows the glow of what it sits next to. But where a beer ad borrows a glow it hasn't earned, here the association is **true**: every card is a hero who earned it through hardship, and players genuinely read themselves into one. That's the differentiator, and it's a moral one. The table must look good — warm light, a night you'd want to be at, not a fluorescent basement.

### Structural defence of the long sad section

The VW "Force" ad is almost entirely failure, and nobody remembers it as sad, because the ending is big enough to rewrite everything before it. The sad part is the setup. The ending just has to hit as hard as the car starting.

And the rescue should be **invisible** — the dad starts the car from the driveway and lets the boy believe he did it. Tex turns up and sets the table without making a thing of it, and Jeff walks out the other side thinking he got there himself.

Belief comes from outside first — the dad moves before the boy believes; Jeff can't choose to be the Fantastic Four while he's still the Hulk. But the acceptance is his own half: the boy still gives it one more try. **Only the invitation makes it a rescue. Both makes it a friendship.**

### Production & distribution

- AI video pass first (Grok/Sora-type) as a moving storyboard and visual brief, so actors know what's being portrayed and can add their own flair. Real actors for the final.
- Shoot the ~2 min version as the hero cut, then carve 90s and 30s. Precedent: JC Penney "Return to the Doghouse".
- Runs on YouTube and on the Legendary Arena site.
- **Soft win:** a demo game played, or a newsletter signup. **Real win:** a paid account.
- Jeff has final say on the edit. Tracy reviews footage.
- Licensors (assuming deals in place) will want: character fidelity to the style guide, Marvel written sign-off on the actual cut, no unapproved third-party branding sharing frame, auditable impression reporting for royalties.

### Crew hats — three tasks each

**Producer** — budget; lock the two houses and the truck; **music rights (the long pole)**.
**Director** — hold the tone guardrails; decide the ending before shooting; rehearse the unspoken subtext.
**Writer** — bring parts one and two up to part three's level; character bibles; lock the long and 90s cuts.
**Cinematographer** — hide his face without it feeling like a gimmick; dark→daylight light plan; design the final arc shot.
**Production designer** — object list; four holiday dressings; source the hero cards.
**Editor** — find the audio-drop beat; cut long first, then carve down; **protect the middle**.
**Music supervisor** — start rights now; commission the solo→harmony arrangement; isolate the opening/closing chord.
**Casting** — the man who can carry scenes from behind; Cindy without coldness; cast the Dealer family as a group.
**First AD** — shooting order around daylight; schedule table redressings; protect rehearsal for the doorway scene.
**Growth/distribution marketer** (a separate hat) — titles, thumbnails, descriptions; paid placement; **make sure the demo is one click, no signup form**.

### Actor subtext (never shown)

Married thirty years, empty nesters in their fifties. He tries and can't do anything right to please her; carries a fear of abandonment. She fears being controlled, so every move closer reads as control and she pulls away.

**She's having an affair and he doesn't know** — actors only, never confirmed to the audience. Plays as small physical beats: phone turned face-down, not wanting him to see the screen.

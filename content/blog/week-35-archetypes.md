Every competitive deck falls into one of four macro archetypes. These are not rigid categories — most decks blend elements — but understanding the pure forms gives you a framework for recognizing what you are building, what your opponents are building, and which archetype best matches the Scenario in front of you.

## Aggro

**Game plan:** Deal damage early, close the game before opponents stabilize or engines come online. Aggro does not build for the late game because it does not intend for a late game to exist.

**Strengths:** Speed. Aggro punishes slow starts, weak openings, and Scenarios that do not pressure the player early. An aggro deck at full speed can close a game before an engine deck finishes assembling.

**Weaknesses:** Runs out of fuel. Aggro decks are front-loaded — powerful early, diminishing late. If the game extends past aggro's closing window, the lack of scaling becomes a liability.

**When to play aggro:** Against Scenarios with fast-escalating threats that reward early aggression. Against Scenarios where the win condition can be achieved quickly. Against opponents who are building slow engines.

**Build principles:** Low curve. High attack density. Minimal economy investment — just enough to recruit efficient attackers. Thin aggressively to see damage cards more often. Close early.

## Engine

**Game plan:** Assemble a draw-and-synergy machine that produces compound turns — turns where each card drawn triggers the next, creating cascading value. Engine decks invest early and dominate late.

**Strengths:** Ceiling. A fully assembled engine produces turns that no other archetype can match. Card draw chains, synergy triggers, and multiplier effects create explosive output that can handle the hardest late-game threats.

**Weaknesses:** Assembly time. Engine decks are weak until the pieces come together. A fast Scenario or an aggro opponent can close the game before the engine activates.

**When to play engine:** Against slow Scenarios that give you time to build. When draw and filtering effects are abundantly available in the recruitment pool. When teammates can stabilize early while you invest.

**Build principles:** Draw effects first. Synergy pieces second. Finishers last. Thin everything that is not part of the engine. Protect the assembly phase with minimal defensive investment.

## Control

**Game plan:** Deny the Scenario its best outcomes. Control decks focus on threat management — preventing escalation, neutralizing dangerous Villains, and stabilizing the board. Control wins slowly, by outlasting the Scenario rather than overpowering it.

**Strengths:** Resilience. Control decks handle the widest range of situations because they are built to respond rather than execute a fixed plan. They are the hardest to disrupt.

**Weaknesses:** Low ceiling. Control decks rarely produce explosive turns. They manage threats efficiently but lack the raw output to close quickly. Games tend to go long.

**When to play control:** Against aggressive Scenarios that require constant threat management. When the Scenario punishes ignoring board presence. When you need to buy time for a teammate's engine to activate.

**Build principles:** Broad threat coverage. Defensive cards and board-management tools. Moderate economy — enough to recruit answers as needed. Flexible rather than focused.

## Hybrid

**Game plan:** Maintain two viable lines — a primary strategy and a [Plan B](/blog/week-43-plan-b/) — so that no single counter can shut you down. Hybrid decks sacrifice peak performance for adaptability.

**Strengths:** Unpredictability. Opponents cannot prepare for a hybrid because they do not know which line you will execute. Scenarios that counter one strategy cannot counter both.

**Weaknesses:** Lower peak performance. A hybrid deck is weaker at its primary strategy than a pure deck because some slots are allocated to the secondary line. Jack of two trades, master of neither.

**When to play hybrid:** In tournaments with diverse Scenario pools where you cannot predict what you will face. Against opponents who hate-draft aggressively and try to disrupt your primary plan.

**Build principles:** Strong core engine that supports both lines. Clearly defined primary and secondary strategies. Flex slots that can shift between lines based on game state.

## Building a tournament roster

For tournament preparation, your three-deck roster should cover at least three of the four archetypes. A common roster:

1. **Main:** Your best archetype. The one you execute most consistently.
2. **Counter:** An archetype that covers matchups where your main struggles.
3. **Flex:** An archetype that adapts to unusual or unexpected Scenarios.

The roster should not be three versions of the same archetype. Diversity is the point — you want tools for different problems, not three hammers.

Until now, you have been learning to build good decks. That was the right focus — a weak deck loses regardless of how well you play it. But a good deck played without regard to the specific game in front of you is still leaving value on the table. Matchup thinking is the shift from "my deck does X" to "this game needs Y, and here is how I deliver it."

## The matchup mindset

Every game is a matchup between your capabilities and the Scenario's demands. The same deck that dominates one Scenario can struggle against another — not because the deck is bad, but because the matchup is unfavorable. Recognizing whether you are in a favorable or unfavorable matchup, and adjusting accordingly, is what separates a player who can build well from a player who can win consistently.

## Reading the matchup before building

Before your first recruit, assess the matchup:

**What does this Scenario reward?** Speed? Control? Flexibility? If the Scenario punishes slow play, a slow engine is a bad matchup regardless of how powerful it is at full capacity.

**What does this Scenario punish?** Narrow strategies? Large hand sizes? Specific card types? If the Scenario has anti-synergy with your preferred approach, you need to adapt or accept the disadvantage.

**What is the win condition?** Not all win conditions are "deal maximum damage." Some Scenarios reward efficiency, board control, or team coordination. Align your build to the actual win condition, not the generic one.

## The three-bullet matchup plan

Before each game, write your matchup plan in three bullets:

1. **My deck's primary goal this game.** (Example: "Build economy fast, then recruit one finisher to close in turns 10–12.")
2. **The Scenario's main threat to that goal.** (Example: "Villain group punishes economy-focused builds with persistent recruit penalties.")
3. **My adjustment.** (Example: "Split early recruits between economy and attack to avoid being fully reliant on recruit generation.")

Three bullets. Thirty seconds. This replaces the default approach of building your favorite deck and hoping for the best.

## Adapting your core strategy

Adaptation does not mean rebuilding from scratch. It means adjusting your proven strategy to account for the specific demands of this game.

**Adjust your curve.** Against fast Scenarios, shift your curve lower — more early-game cards, fewer expensive finishers. Against slow Scenarios, extend your curve higher.

**Adjust your role balance.** Against Scenarios with heavy board presence, add more attack. Against Scenarios with scaling threats, add more control and tempo.

**Adjust your thinning priority.** Against Scenarios that shorten the game, thinning is less valuable (fewer turns to benefit from the improved draw quality). Against long Scenarios, thinning is more valuable.

## Favorable vs unfavorable matchups

**Favorable matchup:** Your strategy naturally answers the Scenario's demands. Your curve aligns, your synergies are relevant, and the threats play into your strengths. In favorable matchups, play your standard game — do not over-adapt. Your base strategy is already correct.

**Unfavorable matchup:** The Scenario counters your strategy or demands something your deck is not built to deliver. In unfavorable matchups, identify the smallest adjustment that moves the matchup from "losing" to "competitive." You cannot turn an unfavorable matchup into a favorable one — but you can narrow the gap.

**Neutral matchup:** Neither advantage nor disadvantage. Neutral matchups are won on execution — the player who plays tighter wins.

## Try this

Before your next three games, write a three-bullet matchup plan. After each game, evaluate: was your matchup assessment accurate? Did your adjustment help or was it unnecessary? Refine the habit of reading the matchup before building.

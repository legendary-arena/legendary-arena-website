There is one card in Legendary Arena that does nothing.

A **Wound** has no power. It gives no recruit, deals no attack, carries no
ability, triggers no combo. Masterminds and villains hand them to you as
damage, and each one slides into your discard pile, gets shuffled into your
deck, and waits there to be drawn — a dead card taking the place of a live
one. There is no clever line of play that turns a Wound into value. It is not
a puzzle with a hidden solution. It is just weight.

Every other card rewards good play. The Wound is the one that can't be played
well at all. And that makes it the most honest card in the game, because it
tells the truth about a certain kind of problem: **some things in your deck
cannot be optimized. They can only be removed.**

## There is no good way to play it

Watch a player who's behind on Wounds and you'll see them do something very
human. They keep the Wound in the deck and try to *play around* it. Draw a bad
hand, shrug, hope the next shuffle is kinder. Build a little bigger to make up
for the dead draws. Tell themselves it's fine, it's only one card.

It is never only one card, because a deck is not a hand — it's the thing every
future hand is drawn from. A Wound left in the deck doesn't cost you one turn.
It quietly taxes **every** turn from now on, thinning each draw a little,
forever, until you take it out.

We do exactly this with the real ones. The grudge, the grief, the habit, the
old guilt — we treat them like cards we can build around. We get good at
playing hands with a wound in them. We call it coping, and coping is a real
skill, but **coping is playing around the wound, not healing it.** The card is
still in the deck. It comes back around every shuffle.

## The productive-looking turn is the trap

Here's why healing is hard, in the game and out of it: removing a Wound never
looks productive.

A heal doesn't advance your board. It doesn't recruit a hero or land a hit or
rescue a bystander. It just takes a bad card out — and next to a turn where you
*did something*, a turn spent only subtracting feels like a wasted one. So the
player keeps choosing the productive-looking move, stays busy, stays efficient,
and never spends the turn that would actually make every hand after it lighter.

The same instinct keeps us from the hard, unglamorous work of clearing a real
wound. Apologizing. Forgiving. Grieving on purpose instead of staying busy.
Sitting with the counselor, or the bishop, or the friend. None of it advances
the board. All of it feels like a turn you can't afford. **But the turn you
spend healing is the only turn that makes every future turn better** — and the
one you keep postponing is the one your whole deck is waiting on.

## It doesn't decay on its own

The tempting lie is that time handles it. Shuffle the Wound to the bottom and
maybe it just... goes away.

It doesn't. A Wound doesn't decay from sitting in the deck. Reshuffled grief
isn't processed grief — it's the same card, coming around again on a longer
loop, and the surprise of drawing it the tenth time hurts about as much as the
first. Left alone, a wound doesn't heal quietly in the dark. It waits.

That's the bad news and, oddly, the good news. Bad, because you don't get to
skip the turn. Good, because the fix is real and available and it *works* — the
Wound doesn't have to be lived with forever. It has a specific removal.

## Sometimes the table heals you

Legendary is a cooperative game, and one of the quiet mercies in its design is
that **you are not always the one who heals your own Wound.** Certain heroes
let a teammate reach across the table and KO a Wound out of *your* deck. Your
recovery can be someone else's action.

That is worth sitting with. There's a stubborn idea that a wound is yours to
carry alone, that asking for help is a kind of losing. The game says the
opposite: a table that lets each other stay clogged with dead cards loses
together, and a table that spends actions healing each other wins together. You
were never meant to draw through it by yourself. Sometimes the most important
move on the board is the one a friend spends on you.

## Removed, not managed

And underneath the friend at the table is the healing the whole idea points
toward. The deepest wounds are not the kind you cope your way out of or even
the kind a friend can lift. Those need the one who "healeth the broken in
heart, and bindeth up their wounds" (Psalm 147:3).

Notice what that promise actually is. Not that He helps you play a better hand
with the Wound still in it — that He *removes* it. Alma, who described his own
guilt as being torn up as if by a harrow, said that when he finally cried out
for mercy, "I could remember my pains no more; yea, I was harrowed up by the
memory of my sins no more" (Alma 36:19). The card didn't get easier to play.
It left the deck.

That's the difference between managing and healing, and it's the difference
the Atonement makes. A managed sin sits at the bottom of the deck, and you
brace for it every shuffle. A forgiven one is gone — "as far as the east is
from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us" (Psalm
103:12). Not buried. Not bottom-decked. Removed from the game.

## The KO pile

That's the image to end on. In Legendary, a healed Wound doesn't go to the
bottom of your deck. It goes to the **KO pile** — out of play, out of the
shuffle, gone. It is never coming around again.

You cannot draw your way out of a wound. You can play brilliantly and lose
anyway if your deck keeps handing you dead cards. The only move that helps is
the one that looks least productive: stop, spend the turn, take the card out.

Then shuffle. The next hand is lighter, and so is every one after it.

Go heal the wound.

Playing games is not the same as practicing. Playing is performing — you use the skills you have to win the game in front of you. Practicing is developing — you isolate a skill and work on it deliberately, even if it means making suboptimal plays in the short term.

Most players never practice. They just play. Then they wonder why they stop improving. This thirty-minute session format gives you a practical routine that fits into a real schedule and produces measurable improvement.

## The three-phase format

### Phase 1: Focused drill (10 minutes)

Choose one skill from the fundamentals or competitive toolkit. Not your strongest skill — your weakest. Isolate it and drill it.

**Drill examples:**

*Sequencing drill:* Play a game where you physically sort every hand left-to-right (draw → synergy → attack → recruit) before playing any card. Focus only on play order, not on winning.

*Threat assessment drill:* Play a game where you verbally rank every threat Tier 1/2/3 before committing any attack. Focus only on prioritization.

*Recruitment discipline drill:* Play a game where you say "keep," "buy," or "skip" out loud for every card in the recruitment row every turn. Focus only on the evaluation.

The drill is not about winning the game. It is about making the skill automatic. You may play poorly because all your attention is on the drill. That is correct. You are investing in long-term improvement at the cost of short-term performance.

### Phase 2: Deliberate play (15 minutes)

Play a game (or a partial game) with full engagement, but with one modification: pause after every major decision and ask "was that the best play?" Not after the game — in the moment.

The pause should be brief — five seconds, not thirty. Just enough to confirm that you are playing intentionally rather than on autopilot. If you catch yourself making a decision without thinking, pause, reconsider, and choose deliberately.

This phase bridges the gap between isolated drills and full-speed play. You are performing, but with a layer of self-awareness that most casual play lacks.

### Phase 3: Targeted review (5 minutes)

After the game, spend five minutes on review. Not a comprehensive game recap — a targeted one.

Answer three questions:

1. **What was the single most impactful decision I made this game?** Not the flashiest — the one that most affected the outcome. Identify it specifically: turn number, the decision, and the alternatives.

2. **Was it correct?** If yes, why? If no, what would have been better? Be specific.

3. **What is my one takeaway for next time?** Write it down. One sentence. "Next time, prioritize draw effects before turn three" or "Next time, check the recruitment row before committing attack." One concrete, actionable adjustment.

Over weeks, your list of takeaways becomes a personal playbook — a record of the lessons that your specific practice sessions revealed.

## Choosing what to practice

Practice your weakest skill, not your favorite strategy. Your favorite strategy is already strong — practicing it feels good but produces diminishing returns. Your weakest skill is where the largest improvement potential lives.

Not sure what your weakest skill is? Review your [Foundations Trial](/blog/week-13-foundations-trial/) or [Synergy Trial](/blog/week-26-synergy-trial/) scores. The lowest-scoring category is your practice target.

## Frequency beats duration

Thirty minutes three times a week produces more improvement than a three-hour session once a week. Frequency reinforces memory and habit formation. Duration produces fatigue and diminishing returns.

If thirty minutes is too much, do fifteen: five-minute drill, eight-minute play, two-minute review. The format scales down. What matters is the structure, not the duration.

## Try this

Run one thirty-minute practice session this week using the three-phase format. Choose your weakest skill for the drill phase. After the session, write down your one takeaway. Do it again next week. After four sessions, review your four takeaways and assess whether your weak skill has improved.

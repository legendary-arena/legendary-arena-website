In multiplayer deckbuilding, the recruitment row is shared. Every card you buy is a card someone else cannot. When two players want the same strategy — the same keyword, the same synergy axis, the same key cards — both decks suffer. Neither reaches critical mass. Both play underpowered versions of the strategy that one player could have executed alone.

Recognizing contested strategies and responding to them is a skill that separates casual players from competitive ones.

## Reading signals

Other players telegraph their strategy through their purchases. You do not need to see their hand — you need to watch what they buy.

**Signal 1: Purchase pattern.** If a player buys two or three cards with the same keyword in their first few turns, they are building around that keyword. If those are the same keywords you are targeting, you are in a contested lane.

**Signal 2: Gap in the row.** When a card you wanted disappears from the recruitment row before your turn, check who took it. If they are buying the same category of cards you need, you are competing for a limited pool.

Early detection matters. The sooner you recognize a contested lane, the sooner you can decide whether to compete or pivot.

## Three responses to a contested lane

### 1. Compete

Stay in the lane and fight for every card. This is correct when:
- You have a significant head start (more cards of that type already acquired)
- The recruitment row refreshes frequently and there are enough cards for both players
- Your deck is already committed and pivoting would cost more than competing

The risk: both decks end up mediocre. Competing only works when the card supply is large enough to support two players or when you are far enough ahead that the other player is the one who should pivot.

### 2. Pivot

Abandon the contested lane and rebuild around a different axis. This is correct when:
- The contest is even and neither player has an advantage
- A viable alternative strategy exists in the current card pool
- You are early enough in the game that pivot cost is low

Pivoting uses the same principles from [Week 9](/blog/week-09-mid-game-pivot/): preserve your engine, change your output. If you built draw effects and economy (which are strategy-neutral), those still work in a different lane. Only your payload needs to change.

### 3. Hate-draft

Buy a card specifically to deny it to your competitor, even if it does not serve your deck. Hate-drafting is the most controversial tactic because it costs you a recruit that could have improved your deck.

Hate-drafting is correct when:
- The card in question would complete your competitor's combo or bring them to critical mass
- Denying it delays their strategy significantly
- The card you take has at least marginal utility in your deck

Hate-drafting is a trap when:
- You spend recruit on a card you will never play, diluting your deck for no benefit
- The competitor can replace the denied card with another from the row
- You hate-draft repeatedly instead of building your own strategy

One surgical hate-draft per game is a tool. Serial hate-drafting is a losing strategy.

## Keeping your own signals ambiguous

If you know to read opponents' signals, they know to read yours. In competitive settings, you can delay telegraphing your strategy:

- Buy neutral cards (draw, economy) early, which work in any strategy
- Avoid heavy keyword commitment in your first three purchases
- Commit to your lane in the mid-game when you have enough information to read the table

This is a trade-off: ambiguity delays your engine's assembly. But it also prevents opponents from targeting your lane with hate-drafts or competition. In tournaments, the information game matters.

## Try this

In your next multiplayer game, track your opponents' first three purchases. Identify what strategy each player is building toward. If your lane is contested, choose your response (compete, pivot, or one hate-draft) consciously rather than by default. After the game, evaluate whether the response was correct.

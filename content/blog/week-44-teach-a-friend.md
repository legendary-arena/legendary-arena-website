The best way to grow the deckbuilding community is to teach one person at a time. But most teaching goes wrong because the teacher tries to explain everything before the new player has touched a card. Rules without context are noise. The ten-minute path gets a new player from zero to "playing and having fun" before their attention runs out.

## The three-phase teach

### Phase 1: Show (3 minutes)

Play one turn yourself while narrating in plain language. Do not explain rules — describe what is happening:

"I draw these five cards. This one gives me two recruit points — that is buying power. This one gives me one attack — that is fighting power. I am going to use my recruit to buy this card from the row because it makes my deck stronger. Then I use my attack on this Villain because defeating it gets it out of the way. Now I discard everything and draw five new cards."

Three minutes. One turn. The new player now understands the rhythm: draw, use resources, buy or fight, discard, draw again.

**What to skip in Phase 1:** Keywords, special abilities, Scheme mechanics, Villain escape consequences. All of these can wait. The only thing that matters is the core loop.

### Phase 2: Guide (4 minutes)

The new player plays their first turn with your guidance. Do not take the cards from them — let them hold the hand, make choices, and physically do the actions. Your job is guardrails:

"What do you want to do with your recruit points? Take a look at the row — anything catch your eye?"

Let them pick a card, even if it is not optimal. Their first buy does not need to be good. It needs to be theirs. Ownership of decisions creates engagement.

If they make a choice that will actively ruin their experience (spending all recruit on a card they cannot afford), gently redirect: "That one costs six and you have three — how about this one instead?" Otherwise, let them explore.

**What to introduce in Phase 2:** How to read card costs and effects (point at the numbers, explain what they mean). One — and only one — special rule that is relevant to their current hand.

### Phase 3: Release (3 minutes)

The new player plays their second and third turns solo. You are available for questions but you do not volunteer information. Let them encounter problems organically.

When they ask "what does this do?" — answer in one sentence. When they ask "what should I buy?" — turn it back: "what does your deck need? Do you want more fighting power or more buying power?" Help them think, not copy.

By the end of Phase 3, the new player has played three turns independently. They understand the core loop. They have made their own decisions. They are playing the game.

## What to explain later

Everything that did not come up in the first ten minutes can wait until it becomes relevant during natural gameplay:

- **Scheme mechanics:** Explain when the first Scheme twist fires. Not before.
- **Villain escapes:** Explain when a Villain is about to escape. Not before.
- **Keyword abilities:** Explain when a keyword card enters their hand. Not before.
- **Advanced strategy:** Not during their first game. Maybe not during their second. Let them discover that there is depth after they discover that the game is fun.

## Making the first game fun

**Let them win.** The first game is not a competition. It is an onboarding experience. If you are playing co-op, carry the team so they can experiment without consequence. If competitive, pull your punches.

**Celebrate their plays.** When they make a smart buy or a good attack, say so. Early positive reinforcement builds the association between deckbuilding and enjoyment.

**Keep it short.** Fifteen to twenty minutes for a first game is ideal. If the game naturally runs long, offer a stopping point: "Want to play one more round or call it here?" A player who ends their first game wanting more will come back. A player who is exhausted from a two-hour session may not.

## Try this

Teach one person this week using the three-phase method. Time each phase. After the game, ask them: "What was your favorite part?" Their answer tells you what to emphasize when you teach the next person.

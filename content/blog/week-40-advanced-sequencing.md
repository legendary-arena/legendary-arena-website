In [Week 5](/blog/week-05-turn-sequencing/), you learned the five-step turn: draw first, evaluate second, commit last. That sequence handles ninety percent of turns. This week is about the other ten percent — the turns where expert-level sequencing means the difference between a good play and the best play.

The expert principle: extract every available piece of information before making any irreversible decision.

## What counts as information

Information is anything that changes your evaluation of the turn. Obvious sources: drawing cards, seeing what is in the recruitment row, reading the board state. Less obvious sources:

**Reveals.** Some effects let you look at the top card of your deck or the top of a Villain stack without drawing or engaging. These reveals provide information at zero cost. Always resolve reveals before committing resources.

**Teammate signals.** In co-op, what your teammate does on their turn provides information about their capabilities. If they defeat a Villain you were considering targeting, you can redirect your attack. Process teammate actions as data before finalizing your plan.

**Scenario feedback.** Some Scenario effects trigger in response to player actions, revealing new information. A Scheme twist that fires mid-turn changes the board state. Incorporate that information before your next action, even if you had already planned it.

## What counts as commitment

A commitment is any action that cannot be undone. Once you spend recruit points, they are gone. Once you allocate attack, it is committed. Once you play a card, its effect resolves.

The expert minimizes commitments per unit of information gathered. Every commitment should happen after every available information source has been exhausted.

## Advanced sequencing patterns

### Pattern 1: The information cascade

Play effects that reveal information in order of information value. A card that lets you look at the top three cards of your deck tells you more than a card that draws one. Resolve the high-information effect first, then use that information to decide whether and how to use the draw effect.

### Pattern 2: Conditional branching

Some turns present a fork: two mutually exclusive paths, each leading to a different outcome. Before committing to a branch, check whether any available action would clarify which branch is superior. A reveal effect, a draw, even a teammate's action might resolve the uncertainty.

If no action can clarify, commit to the higher-floor branch — the one with the better worst-case outcome.

### Pattern 3: Delayed commitment

When you have multiple actions to take, sequence the reversible ones first and the irreversible ones last. Playing a card that draws is reversible (you can still choose what to do with the drawn cards). Spending recruit to buy is irreversible (the recruit is gone). Always draw before spending.

This extends to attack: if you have optional attack targets and some attacks trigger additional effects, sequence the attack that reveals information first (defeating a Villain that flips a new card) before committing remaining attacks.

## Common expert-level mistakes

**Committing before counting.** Playing a synergy-triggering card before verifying how many triggers are available. Count first, then play.

**Resolving out of order.** Playing effects in the order they appear in your hand rather than the order that maximizes information. Physically reorder your hand by information value.

**Ignoring partial information.** A card effect that reveals one card from a hidden source is partial information. It does not tell you everything, but it tells you something. Use it.

## Try this

In your next game, before each commitment (recruit, attack, card play), ask: "Is there any action I could take first that would give me information to make this commitment better?" If yes, take the information action first. Track how many times delayed commitment changed your decision.

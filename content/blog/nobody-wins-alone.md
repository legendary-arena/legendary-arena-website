Legendary is a cooperative game, and that word does more work than it looks
like it does.

It means there is one mastermind, one scheme, and one table — and there is no
private win condition hiding inside it. The mastermind falls to the team or it
doesn't fall at all. The scheme, if it runs its plot to the end, defeats
*everyone* at once, in the same instant, regardless of how well any single seat
was playing. You do not get to win your own little game at a losing table. The
outcome has everyone's name on it.

There's a scoreboard on top of that — once the mastermind is down, the game
sorts who contributed what. But the sorting only happens **after** the shared
result. Win first, together; get ranked second. A brilliant personal game
inside a team loss doesn't rank low. It doesn't rank at all, because the match
was lost, and the match is the thing.

## You cannot out-turn a collapse

This is the math that should change how you play. **Your best turn is worth
nothing if the scheme wins.** There is no hand so good it out-scores a lost
match, no engine so large it keeps running after the table falls. You cannot
carry a co-op game the way you can carry a solo one, because the failure
condition isn't yours to hold off alone — it belongs to the seat under the most
pressure, and that seat might not be yours.

Ecclesiastes put the whole co-op principle down three thousand years before
there were card games to illustrate it: "Two are better than one... For if they
fall, the one will lift up his fellow: **but woe to him that is alone when he
falleth; for he hath not another to help him up**" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). The
lone player, however strong, has no one to lift him when he falls — and at a
co-op table, when he falls, so do you.

## The strongest play is often on someone else's board

Which leads to the move new players almost never make and good ones make
constantly: **spending your turn on a teammate.**

Heal the wound clogging their deck. Hand them the hero they can use better than
you can. Cover the villain about to overrun *their* side of the city, not
yours. None of it advances your personal position, and all of it can be the
best play on the board — because the table doesn't win at its ceiling, it
survives at its floor, and the floor is whichever seat is closest to breaking.
Raise that seat and you've raised the whole table's odds more than another point
of your own ever could.

That is what "**Bear ye one another's burdens**" (Galatians 6:2) looks like
rendered into rules. Not a sentiment — a strategy. The burden that's about to
sink the table is the one worth spending your turn to lift, even when it isn't
yours, *especially* when it isn't yours, because in a game with one shared
outcome, your neighbor's burden already is yours. You just haven't felt it land
yet.

## There is no "not my problem" seat

Feel it land, though, and you'll notice the game keeps insisting on it. The
Master Strike hits *every* player, not just the active one. The scheme's clock
is everyone's clock. An escaping villain and an advancing plot threaten the
whole table at once. The board is built so that no seat can wall itself off and
tend its own garden while the others burn — pressure applied anywhere is
pressure on everyone.

Paul described the same architecture in the body of Christ: "**whether one
member suffer, all the members suffer with it; or one member be honoured, all
the members rejoice with it**" (1 Corinthians 12:26). That is a co-op table
exactly. There is no seat whose suffering stays local and no victory whose joy
belongs to one player. The hand can't tell the foot its trouble isn't the
hand's problem. It is the same body, and it has one fate.

## The private scoreboard is a trap

So here is the temptation, and it's a real one because the scoreboard *is*
real: play your own score. Chase your victory pile, optimize your line, build
your impressive turn — while the table's actual position quietly rots. You can
absolutely do this. You can post the top individual number in a match your team
loses.

And it means nothing, because the number was always downstream of the shared
win. A high score in a lost match is a footnote to a defeat. The player heads-
down on his own tally while the scheme ticks toward its end has mistaken the
ranking for the game — he's polishing his contribution to a loss. The discipline
is the opposite instinct: watch the table's floor, not your own ceiling, and
spend accordingly. You are not trying to be the best player at a table that
loses. You are trying to be part of a table that wins.

## The win is a "we"

Listen to how anyone talks after a real co-op game and you'll hear the whole
lesson in the pronoun. Nobody says "I beat the mastermind." They say "*we* got
there" — we held the left side, we survived the Strike on turn six, we pulled it
out. The victory is grammatically plural because it was actually plural. There
is no sentence in this game where you defeat the mastermind by yourself, because
there is no such event.

That's the quiet argument the whole cooperative design is making, turn after
turn: **the good you're playing for is a shared good, and it does not come to
you one seat at a time.** You are saved together or not at all. Bear the burden
that isn't yours. Lift the seat that's falling. Spend the turn on your neighbor.

Then win the only way the game lets you — together — and notice, when it's
over, that the games you actually remember are never the ones where you had the
best score. They're the ones you got through with someone beside you.

Nobody wins alone. Play like it.

Power is how hard you hit. Tempo is when you hit. A player with less raw power but better tempo often wins because they act before the Scenario's threats can escalate. Tempo is the art of staying ahead of the clock — not just accumulating resources, but deploying them at the moment they have maximum impact.

## Proactive vs reactive play

Reactive play responds to threats as they appear. A Villain shows up, you fight it. A Scheme twist fires, you deal with the fallout. Reactive play feels solid — you are handling problems as they arrive.

Proactive play eliminates threats before they become problems. You defeat a Villain the turn it appears, before its persistent effect activates. You invest in board control before the Scheme reaches a twist threshold. Proactive play costs the same resources as reactive play but produces better outcomes because you prevent damage rather than repair it.

The difference between reactive and proactive play is tempo. Tempo means you act first.

## The tempo equation

Every turn, the Scenario adds threats and you respond. The Scenario has a tempo of its own — a rate at which pressure increases. Your job is to match or exceed that rate.

When your tempo exceeds the Scenario's, the board gets cleaner each turn. Threats are removed faster than they appear. You build a lead that compounds.

When the Scenario's tempo exceeds yours, the board gets dirtier. Threats accumulate, penalties stack, and you fall into a reactive spiral where every turn is spent putting out fires instead of advancing your position.

## Playing for denial

Tempo is not just about speed. It is about denial — removing the Scenario's best options before they activate.

A Villain with a powerful persistent effect is worth defeating immediately, even if you could deal with it later. Every turn that effect is active, it costs you something. Defeating it now denies those future costs. The attack you spend this turn is an investment in every future turn.

The denial lens changes your priorities. Instead of asking "what is the biggest threat?" you ask "what will cost me the most if I leave it alone for one more turn?" Those are different questions, and the denial question often points to a different target.

## When to invest in tempo over power

Sometimes a weaker card with an immediate effect is worth more than a stronger card you will not use for several turns. A three-cost attacker that lets you defeat a Villain this turn is tempo-positive. A six-cost finisher that sits in your deck for five turns before appearing is power-positive but tempo-negative.

Invest in tempo when:
- The Scenario escalates rapidly and delay is punished
- Early threats have persistent effects that compound
- You need to buy time for an [engine](/blog/week-15-engine-building/) to assemble

Invest in power when:
- The Scenario starts slow and you have breathing room
- Late-game threats require raw damage that tempo cards cannot provide
- Your engine is already online and the question is output, not timing

## Tempo in co-op

In cooperative play, tempo is a team resource. One player investing in tempo (clearing threats early, controlling the board) benefits the entire team by reducing the pressure on everyone else. The [Stabilizer role](/blog/week-12-coop-roles/) is essentially the team's tempo manager.

Coordinate: if the Stabilizer is handling tempo, other players can safely invest in power. If no one is managing tempo, the team's collective position degrades every turn.

## Try this

In your next game, make one decision each turn based on denial rather than power. Choose a target not because it is the most threatening right now, but because leaving it alone for another turn would cost the most. Track whether denial-first targeting changed the trajectory of the game.

Every co-op match in Legendary Arena now carries a **Battle Plan** — one
shared document for the whole team, in three phases tied to the arc of the
game:

1. **Pre-battle plan**, written in the waiting room before a card is drawn:
   here is the mastermind, here are the villains, here is why we picked these
   heroes.
2. **Battle adjustments**, edited live while you play: who's carrying the
   board now, where the line changed, what we're focusing next.
3. **Post-battle analysis**, filled in once the game is over and sits beside
   the report card: what actually worked.

It's a small feature. But the shape of it — plan, adjust, debrief, in that
order — is worth more than the pixels it takes up, because the order is the
part almost everyone gets backwards.

## The parked car

Start with the objection, because it comes up first. Committing to a plan
before you're certain it's the right one sounds reckless. Shouldn't you wait
until you know?

My bishop has a line for this: **the Lord can't steer a parked car.** You
don't get corrected by standing still. Direction is something that happens to
things already in motion — a wheel that isn't turning can't be turned. The
person waiting for certainty before they commit isn't being careful. They're
being un-steerable.

The waiting room proves it in miniature. You can sit there refining a
pre-battle plan forever and it accomplishes exactly nothing until someone
starts the match. The plan doesn't become real until the car is moving.

## What you're allowed to change

Here's the distinction the three phases quietly teach. **Revising a plan
means adjusting the *how*. Abandoning it means changing the *what*.** Those
are not the same act, and confusing them is where most plans die.

Battle adjustments — phase two — are all *how*. The destination never moves:
you're still trying to win this game, against this mastermind. What changes is
the route. You committed to a line, the twist disrupted it, so you shift the
focus and keep driving toward the same finish. That's revision, and a good
team does it constantly.

Changing the *what* is a different thing entirely, and the game punishes it
the same way life does. The player who keeps re-deciding which win condition
they're chasing — economy this turn, aggro the next, board control after that
— never gets the one thing that actually wins: compounding. Every pivot resets
the clock. So the honest rule is short: **change your methods often, change
your destination rarely.**

Think about it in the plainest possible terms. Revising your plan is a
student changing *how* they study. Switching majors every single year is a
student changing *what* they're studying — and that student graduates with
nothing but a very diverse transcript. The methods are supposed to move. The
destination is supposed to hold.

## Madison's draft was good enough

The cleanest example of holding the destination isn't from a card game. It's
from a hot room in Philadelphia in 1787.

James Madison walked into the Constitutional Convention with the Virginia
Plan already drafted. He had done the reading beforehand — years of it — and
then he committed to a framework and handed it to the room. **He did not go
back and redraft it.** He didn't return the next week with a monarchy to try
instead, then a pure democracy, then something else he'd read about over the
weekend. He held the destination and let the committee adjust the route.

That's why there's a republic. Imagine the other Madison — the one who keeps
starting over, convinced the next draft will finally be the certain one. He'd
have been the best-read man on the beach, a library in his head and nothing
built on the sand. Endless drafts, no republic.

The trap he avoided is the one that's easiest to fall into, because it
disguises itself as diligence: **research feels like progress.** Reading one
more book, considering one more option, refining the plan one more time — it
all feels like work, and none of it moves the car. Madison did the research
*first*, committed to one draft, and then let a good-enough plan get better in
contact with reality. A draft the committee can improve beats a perfect draft
that never leaves your desk.

## Alma's tree

There's a patience underneath all of this that's easy to skip.

Alma taught faith as a seed you plant and nourish. The part people forget is
how long it takes and how gently you have to treat it. **Pruning a branch is
care. Digging up the roots every week to check on them kills the tree.** A
plan is the same organism. Adjusting a method is pruning. Tearing the whole
thing up because you're anxious about whether it's working yet is root-digging,
and it's fatal for the same reason.

Alma's promise is that the reward comes on the tree's schedule, not yours —
"by and by ye shall pluck the fruit thereof," after "your diligence and your
faith and your patience with the word in nourishing it" (Alma 32:42). The
fruit is real. It is just later than you'd like, and demanding it early is how
you lose it.

## You won't go too far down the wrong path

The last piece is the reassurance the whole thing needs, because otherwise
"commit before you're certain" really would be reckless.

You commit to a direction knowing you'll be corrected as you move. Nephi went
over the wall to get the plates "led by the Spirit, **not knowing beforehand**
the things which I should do" (1 Nephi 4:6) — he went in without the plan
fully formed and got the next step once he was moving. The brother of Jared
built the barges, sealed them, and then handed the steering to the wind and
the waves; the furious wind that must have felt like the wrong direction was
the thing carrying them to the promised land.

That's the promise: get the destination right, commit, and the course
corrections arrive *as you go*. But only as you go. You cannot be redirected
while you're parked. The wind can't carry a boat that's still lashed to the
dock, and the Spirit's "not knowing beforehand" is only useful to someone
who's already climbing the wall.

## The headlights

I'll close with the best image, which isn't mine either.

In the old days of cars, the headlights ran off the engine directly — so the
faster you drove, the brighter they burned. Crawl along and you could barely
see the road. Open it up and the light reached ahead of you. **The light came
with the speed.**

That's the whole thing. Stand still and you see almost nothing — which is
exactly what the man on the beach with his perfect library experienced. Move,
and the road lights up as far as you need it to, one stretch at a time.

So: write the pre-battle plan. Commit to a line. Adjust the *how* as often as
the game demands and hold the *what* until the game is over. Then fill in the
debrief and do it again, a little better.

Start the match. The headlights come on when you do.

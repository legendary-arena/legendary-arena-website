Variance is a fact of deckbuilding. You shuffle a deck, you draw randomly, and sometimes the cards come in the wrong order. You cannot eliminate variance. But you can manage it — reducing its impact on your results so that skill, not luck, determines who wins.

The counterintuitive truth: high-rolling — those spectacular turns where everything comes together perfectly — is often a sign that your deck is too volatile. If your best turns are extraordinary, your worst turns are probably terrible. Consistent players trade the highs for a higher floor, and the higher floor wins more games.

## Single points of failure

A single point of failure is any element your strategy depends on that, when absent, causes your deck to collapse. Common single points of failure:

**One critical card.** If your strategy only works when you draw one specific card, every hand without it is a dead hand. In a thirty-card deck, you miss it more often than you see it.

**One specific combo.** If your damage output depends on two cards appearing together, you have compounded the draw probability problem. Two specific cards in the same hand is significantly less likely than one.

**One resource type.** If your entire deck depends on recruit generation, and a Scenario effect disrupts recruit, your deck is offline.

Eliminate single points of failure by building [redundancy](/blog/week-23-consistency/). Multiple cards that serve the same critical function. Multiple paths to the same outcome. No single card that you absolutely must draw.

## The risk/reward matrix

Every decision in a deckbuilding game has a risk/reward profile:

**Low risk, low reward.** Safe plays that make small progress. Example: recruiting a solid mid-range card that fits your strategy.

**Low risk, high reward.** The best plays in the game. Example: recruiting a card that is both individually strong and synergizes with your existing deck.

**High risk, high reward.** Gambles. Example: skipping defense to recruit an expensive finisher, hoping the Scenario does not escalate next turn.

**High risk, low reward.** Traps. Example: hate-drafting a card you will never use to deny an opponent who could find an alternative anyway.

The decision rule is simple: take low-risk plays by default. Take high-risk plays only when:
- You are behind and safe plays cannot recover your position
- The potential reward is so large that it changes the game trajectory
- The downside is survivable (a bad outcome delays you but does not eliminate you)

When you are ahead, never take high-risk plays. The percentage line wins from a lead.

## Controlling what you can control

Three things you can control:

1. **Deck construction.** Build for consistency. Redundancy, thinning, filtering — all the tools from [Week 23](/blog/week-23-consistency/).
2. **Sequencing.** Play draw effects first. Maximize information before commitments. Follow the [five-step turn](/blog/week-05-turn-sequencing/).
3. **Decision framework.** Apply the risk/reward matrix to every choice. Do not gamble when safe plays are available.

Three things you cannot control:

1. **Draw order.** Shuffle is random. Accept it.
2. **Scenario events.** Twist reveals and Villain appearances are outside your control.
3. **Opponent actions.** You can read and predict, but you cannot dictate.

Invest your energy in the controllable. Accept the uncontrollable. Players who rage at bad draws waste mental energy that could be spent on clean execution.

## Try this

After your next game, review each turn and classify every major decision as low-risk or high-risk. Count the high-risk decisions. Were any of them unnecessary — situations where a safe play was available but you chose to gamble? In your next game, reduce your high-risk count by one.

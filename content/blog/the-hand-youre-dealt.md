A deck-builder makes you do two completely different things and quietly dares
you to confuse them.

The first is **building the deck.** Over the game you recruit heroes and add
them to your deck — every card in it is there because at some point you chose to
put it there. That is total control. Nothing enters your deck that you didn't
select.

The second is **drawing your hand.** Each turn you draw off the top, and you do
not choose what comes up. The shuffle decides. You can have built a magnificent
deck and still draw its five weakest cards this turn, or its five best. The one
thing you never get to do — not this turn, not any turn — is pick which cards
arrive in your hand.

Two different acts. Two different kinds of control, total and none. And nearly
everything about playing well, in the game and out of it, comes down to not
mixing them up.

## "I drew badly"

Listen at any table and you'll hear the most common lament in the whole hobby:
*I drew badly.* The engine never came together, the answer sat at the bottom of
the deck, the hand was dead. All true, and all aimed at exactly the one thing
that was never the lever.

The draw was never yours to choose. Being frustrated at your hand is being
frustrated at the shuffle — at the one part of the game no player's hand ever
touches. It's the purest waste of a turn there is, because while you're
resenting the cards that came up, the actual levers — the two places your choice
lives — are sitting right there untouched.

## Where the agency actually is

Your agency is real. It's just spent earlier and later than the draw, and
people keep looking for it in the exact spot where it isn't.

**Upstream, it lives in composition.** Every card you recruit is a vote about
who your future self will be. You're not choosing this hand; you're choosing the
*shape of every hand you'll ever draw* — the odds, the ceiling, the floor, the
character of the deck. That is enormous power. It's the power to decide what
kind of turns are even possible for you, and it's exercised one small,
unglamorous recruit at a time, long before the hand that needs it comes up. The
deck you're drawing from today is a monument to choices you already made.

**Downstream, it lives in the play.** Given the hand — brilliant or terrible —
there is a best line through it, and finding it is the second place you're free.
The dead hand still has a least-bad sequence. The great hand can still be
misplayed. Between "what I drew," which is not yours, sit "what I built," which
was yours, and "how I play this," which is yours right now.

Miss both of those and stare at the draw, and you've handed away the whole game
to the one part of it you were never holding.

## The servant who buried his talent

There's a parable built on exactly this shape. A master gives three servants
different amounts — "to every man according to his several ability." Nobody
chose his allotment; the amounts were dealt. What they were judged on, entirely,
was what they did with the hand they were given.

Two of them traded and doubled what they had, and heard the line everyone hopes
to hear: "**Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful
over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things**" (Matthew 25:21).
The third committed the deck-builder's cardinal sin. He looked at his hand,
decided it was too little to matter, blamed the one who dealt it, and buried it
in the ground. He played no line at all. And he was the one condemned — not for
drawing badly, but for doing nothing with what he drew.

Notice the standard. **Faithfulness is measured over the given, never over the
ideal.** The commendation isn't "well done, you had the best cards." It's "you
were faithful over a *few* things" — you played the modest hand you were dealt,
and played it well. "It is required in stewards," Paul says, "that a man be
found faithful" (1 Corinthians 4:2) — faithful with the deck he was handed, not
resentful that it wasn't a different one.

## Two ways to get it wrong

There are two errors here, and they're mirror images.

The first is **fatalism** — the one-talent servant's error. The draw decides
everything, so why bother; the hand's bad, so the turn's lost; my
circumstances were dealt, so my life is just playing out. This buries the
talent. It treats the shuffle as the whole game and quietly surrenders the two
levers you actually hold.

The second is the **illusion of control** — raging at the draw as though a
louder complaint could have changed which cards came up. This wastes the turn a
different way: it spends your one real resource, the hand in front of you *now*,
on relitigating a hand you were never going to choose.

The mature player holds all three truths at once, and they don't fight: **I
chose this deck** — so own it, improve it, keep recruiting well. **I did not
choose this hand** — so stop cursing it. **I choose how I play it, right now** —
so get on with the best line available. Build faithfully, accept the draw,
play wisely. It's the serenity to accept the shuffle and the agency to do
everything on either side of it.

## An agent, not a hand

Underneath it is the oldest thing that can be said about a person: you are one
who *acts*, not merely one who is *acted upon*. That is what agency means. We
are, Lehi taught, "free to choose liberty and eternal life... or to choose
captivity and death" (2 Nephi 2:27) — free, which is to say the choosing is
genuinely ours. The draw acts on you. Your building and your playing are how you
act back. A life spent cursing the shuffle is a life lived as an object — as a
hand something else deals — when the whole dignity of the thing is that you were
never only that.

## Play it well

So here is the shape of it, and it's worth getting straight before the next turn
comes up. You will not get to choose your hand. That was never on the table, not
today and not any day. But the deck it's drawn from is a record of choices that
were entirely yours — and it's still being built, so the next recruit is another
one. And the hand you're holding right now, whatever came up, has a best line
through it that no shuffle can take away from you.

The good and faithful servant was never the one dealt the most. He was the one
who traded faithfully with what he was handed. You don't get to choose your
hand. You chose your deck, and you choose your play — and that is where all the
freedom you actually have has been sitting the whole time.

Play it well.

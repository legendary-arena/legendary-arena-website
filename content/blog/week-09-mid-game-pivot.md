You built an economy engine. You planned for a big mid-game power spike. And then the Scenario threw something at you that made your plan irrelevant. The recruitment row shifted. A key card got snatched by another player. The threat you prepared for was replaced by something your deck cannot handle.

Now you have a choice: stay the course and hope it works out, or pivot. Both options have costs. Staying risks irrelevance. Pivoting risks losing the investment you already made. The skill is knowing which risk is smaller.

## The sunk cost trap

The most common reason players refuse to pivot is sunk cost. "I already invested three turns building economy — I cannot abandon that now." But those three turns are spent regardless. The question is not whether the investment was worthwhile. The question is whether continuing to invest will pay off given what you know now.

If your economy engine is strong but the threats now require a specific type of attack you do not have, more economy does not help. The engine is fine. The output is wrong. Pivoting your output while keeping your engine is not abandoning your investment — it is redirecting it.

## Pivot triggers

Four signals that your plan needs adjustment:

### 1. Key cards are unavailable

Your strategy depended on acquiring specific cards from the recruitment row, and those cards are gone — bought by opponents or never appearing. If your plan requires cards you cannot get, no amount of patience will fix it.

### 2. The threat profile changed

The Scenario escalated in an unexpected direction. A Villain group entered that counters your build. A Scheme twist altered the win condition. Your deck is still doing what it was built to do — but what it was built to do no longer matters.

### 3. A teammate shifted role

In co-op, your partner pivoted to cover the role you were filling. Now two players are doing the same job and another role is uncovered. One of you needs to adjust, and the one with fewer cards invested in the overlapping role should be the one to move.

### 4. You are losing and the cause is structural

Falling behind can be tactical (a bad draw, a tough matchup) or structural (your deck cannot handle this game). Tactical deficits recover on their own. Structural deficits compound. If you identify that the problem is your deck's architecture and not your luck, pivoting is the only path forward.

## How to pivot without losing tempo

A full pivot — abandoning your strategy and starting over — is almost never correct. You cannot unrecruit the cards you already bought. The goal is a partial pivot: changing your output while preserving your foundation.

**Step 1: Identify what still works.** Your economy cards still generate recruit. Your draw effects still provide cards. The engine does not need to change — the payload does.

**Step 2: Define the new output.** What does the game need from you now? More attack? Different attack types? Board control? Define the new goal in one sentence, just like your original [strategy sentence](/blog/week-01-deck-checklist/).

**Step 3: Recruit for the new output.** Starting immediately, recruit cards that serve the new sentence. Ignore cards that serve the old one, even if they are technically good. You are building a second layer on top of an existing foundation.

**Step 4: Accept transition cost.** For two to three turns after the pivot, your deck will underperform. It has old-plan cards that no longer contribute optimally and new-plan cards that have not yet appeared in your draws. This is the cost of pivoting. It is real, but it is temporary. The cost of not pivoting — a deck that never matches the game it is playing — is permanent.

## When to stay the course

Not every setback warrants a pivot. Stay the course when:

- **The problem is temporary.** A bad draw sequence is not a broken strategy. If your engine is intact and the threats are manageable, play through it.
- **The game is almost over.** Pivoting costs tempo. If the game will end in three turns, the transition cost is higher than the benefit. Play what you have and maximize your existing tools.
- **Your teammates have it covered.** If the new threat is real but your partner is better positioned to handle it, let them adjust. Not every problem is your problem to solve.

## Try this

After your next game, identify the moment where a pivot was either taken or should have been taken. What was the trigger? What changed in the output? How many turns did the transition cost? If you stayed the course when you should have pivoted, estimate what pivoting earlier would have changed.

Players practice playing from behind far more than playing from ahead, because being behind feels urgent and being ahead feels comfortable. But more games are lost from winning positions than most players realize. The lead creates a false sense of security that invites risk-taking, lazy execution, and the slow erosion of advantage until the game slips away.

The lock pattern is a framework for converting a lead into inevitability.

## Why players throw leads

Three patterns explain most thrown leads:

**Greed.** You are ahead, so you take a risk to get further ahead — an ambitious recruit, a speculative attack allocation, a setup turn when you should be closing. The risk does not pay off, and the turn you wasted gave the Scenario time to escalate.

**Relaxation.** The pressure feels lower when you are ahead. Decision-making gets lazy. You play on autopilot instead of evaluating each turn fresh. Small mistakes accumulate until the lead is gone.

**Over-building.** You keep recruiting because your economy generates recruit points and the recruitment row has appealing cards. But every card added to your deck at this stage is [dilution](/blog/week-24-dilution/). Your deck was winning at its current size. Making it bigger does not make it better — it makes it slower.

## The lock pattern

A lock is a game state where the Scenario cannot recover. You have enough output to close, enough defense to survive, and enough consistency to execute for the remaining turns. The lock pattern has three components:

### 1. Deny comeback paths

Identify how the Scenario could reverse your lead. What would need to happen for your position to collapse? A specific Villain escape, a Scheme twist threshold, a board-state tipping point. Then allocate resources to prevent those specific outcomes.

You do not need to fight every threat when you are ahead. You need to fight the threats that could erase your lead. Everything else is noise.

### 2. Stop investing, start converting

When you are ahead, every turn should convert existing advantage into progress toward the win condition. Stop recruiting. Stop building your engine. The engine is done. Use it.

The conversion test: does this action move me closer to winning, or does it make my deck "better"? If the answer is "better," you are over-building. If the answer is "closer to winning," you are converting.

### 3. Play the percentage line

When ahead, take the safest line that still closes the game. Do not pursue the optimal line if it involves risk. Do not try for the biggest possible turn if a slightly smaller turn wins without danger.

The percentage line asks: what is the play that wins in the highest percentage of scenarios? That play is almost always conservative when you have a lead. Save the bold plays for when you are behind and need to gamble.

## Identifying the lock condition

Before each turn, check: is the lock achievable this turn? The lock condition is specific to each game, but it generally looks like:

- Your damage output per turn exceeds the Scenario's remaining win threshold divided by the number of turns you have
- No single Scenario event can reverse your position
- Your deck consistency ensures you can deliver the required output reliably

When all three conditions are met, you are in lock. Play the remaining turns mechanically — clean, safe, efficient.

## The close-out checklist

When ahead, run through this before each turn:

1. Can I close the game this turn? If yes, do it.
2. If not, what is the closest I can get?
3. What Scenario event could disrupt my lead? Can I prevent it?
4. Am I recruiting? If so, why? (The answer should almost never be "because I can.")

## Try this

In your next game where you take a lead, consciously identify the moment you should stop building and start closing. Track whether you recruited any cards after that moment. If you did, evaluate whether those recruits helped you win or simply made a winning deck bigger.

> God doesn't want us to *have* something. He wants us to *be* something.
> Satan doesn't want us to *be* something. He wants us to *have* something.

For thirty years I could have told you who I was in three words.
Husband. Father. Engineer. It never occurred to me that all three were
things someone else had handed me — and that anything handed to you can
be handed back.

I want to tell this the way I understand it now, not the way it felt
then, because that is the only honest way I have it. The man who lived it
was frightened. The man telling it is grateful. Both of us are telling
you the truth.

## One

You can be married and alone. I was, for a long time before anyone would
have called it a marriage ending. We didn't fight. Fighting would have
meant we were still trying to reach each other. Most evenings Cindy was
on her side of the couch with her earbuds in, her face lit by a screen I
wasn't on, and I'd say something and watch my words arrive a beat after
my hand had crossed into the light. She didn't look up. It wasn't
cruelty. We were two people in the same room who had stopped meeting
there, and were polite about it.

I hadn't understood yet that every name I answered to could be taken
back. Cindy would end the husband. The children had grown up and thinned
out the father. Retirement was coming for the engineer. I had spent
fifty years becoming a set of roles, and I had never once looked
underneath them to see whether anyone was there.

These were fish handed to me. Nobody had taught me to fish.

When she left, I looked underneath the roles. And for a while, there was
nothing there at all.

## Two

I kept the pension. I didn't keep the house.

There's a particular kind of bottom that nobody is cruel to you at. That
was mine. People were kind. They were busy, and useful, and kind, and
they had their own lives to get back to, and I stayed in the spare room
of one of them with my things still in bags because unpacking felt like
agreeing to something.

Weeks went by. I told myself I was adjusting. I was not adjusting. I was
waiting — for the phase to end, for something to arrive, for whatever it
is you decide is required before you're allowed to start again.

I've since made my peace with the man beside the pool of Bethesda, who
had been waiting thirty-eight years for the water to move. When the Lord
finds him He asks one question — *Wilt thou be made whole?* — and the man
doesn't answer it. He explains. He tells Him why it has never worked, how
he has no one to put him in, how someone always steps down ahead of him.
And here is the part that undid me when I finally saw it: he was right.
Every word of it was true. Nobody did put him in. And being right had
kept him on that mat for thirty-eight years.

I knew that man because I was becoming him. My excuses were true, too —
that's what made them work. I really had lost the house. She really had
been the one to leave. A true reason is the best hiding place there is,
because you can't argue with it. You can sit behind it for a lifetime and
call it patience.

The brother of Jared sat in his tents by the sea for four years before
the Lord came to him, and when He came, He was displeased — not for a sin
the man had committed, but for the prayer he hadn't prayed. The barges
only got built after the waiting stopped. I understand now that I was on
that beach. I just had better excuses than most for staying on it.

## Three

Tex didn't give me advice. That's the whole reason it worked.

He knew what had happened — everyone did — and he never once asked me how
I was holding up, or told me what he'd have done, or handed me a book.
One evening he just said there was a game on Thursday, and that I should
come.

*Ask, seek, knock.* I have thought a great deal about the order of those
words. Every one of them is a verb, and every one costs more than the
last — asking is a word, seeking is time, knocking puts you at another
man's door. Not *wait*. Not *rest*. Not *be still and it will find you.*
Tex knocked first — grace nearly always runs out ahead of the asking. But
the door was mine to open, and no one could open it for me.

My half of it was smaller than you'd think a turning point should be. I
picked up my keys. That was all it asked of me — not to be healed, not to
be ready, not to feel like a man who goes to a card game. Just to put the
keys in my pocket and drive over. I barely spoke that first night. I
didn't understand what I was doing there. It didn't matter. I came.

## Four

Then came the long part. The part the story is actually about, and the
part I'd have skipped if I were selling you something.

Thursdays stacked up. Most of them were unremarkable, which is the point
— you don't change on the remarkable days. There were bad weeks I nearly
didn't come, and one or two I sat in the truck in the dark in the parking
lot first, because a grown man crying is easier to do where no one can
see it, and then I wiped my face and walked in as if I were fine. Her
name came up on my phone one night mid-game and I turned it face-down on
the table without deciding to, the same gesture she used to make at me,
and I never did find out why she called.

I drove past a bar on the way there every week and thought about how much
simpler the evening would be inside it. I sat, most weeks, next to a man
who came every Thursday too and told the same story about his ex-wife
every Thursday and had not moved an inch in all the years he'd been
telling it. He was the Bethesda man made flesh, right there at the table.
No one judged him. He was right about everything that had happened to
him. Being right, it turns out, is still not the same as being free.

Alma says you plant a seed and you don't get fruit the next morning, and
that if you neglect it, it won't matter how good the seed was. But he
also says you know it is good long before there is anything to eat —
because it *swells*, because it begins to sprout and grow, and that
swelling is enough to go on. That was the size of my weekly reward. Not a
harvest. Just the smallest proof that something in me was still alive.

Nobody at that table taught me anything. That's not modesty — it's the
mechanism. Scripture is full of men who came out the far side of their
becoming without a single verse for the middle of it. Enoch's whole city
is redeemed in five words — *in process of time* — generations of unseen
work compressed to nothing, because no one is meant to watch a man become
someone. The practice is always invisible. Nobody sees the thousand wrong
notes; they only ever hear you play. The roots of the olive tree do their
work in the dark, reaching down, while everyone waits on the fruit.

So no one saw my year, and I couldn't see it either. If you'd asked me,
in the middle of it, whether I was becoming someone, I'd have said I was
killing a Thursday. But you cannot show up somewhere for a year, badly,
and stay the same man. The whole time I thought I was waiting, I was
already down in the dark growing roots.

That's the part they'll want to cut, whoever tells this after me. The
long unglamorous middle where nothing obviously happens. Don't let them.
The length *is* the meaning. If Tex had turned up in week two, it would
have been a kind gesture. A year in, it was the thing that saved my life.

## The other road

I should tell you about Cindy, because she isn't the villain of this and
she isn't a subplot. She had the same wound I did.

Her identity was mother the way mine was father, and when the children
left, she looked underneath and found the same nothing. Same crisis. We
answered it differently, and the difference was patience. I waited in the
dark until someone knocked, and then I grew something slowly. She wanted
the harvest that season — or that is how it looked from where I stood, and
I have learned not to be too certain about a heart that stopped telling me
things a long time before either of us left.

That's the oldest counterfeit there is. Chop the tree down to get at the
fruit now. Kill the goose for all the eggs at once. Ask the father for
the inheritance early — which is only another way of wishing him already
gone, so you can *have* the thing today instead of waiting for it to come
to you rightly. The younger son does exactly that, spends it all on
riotous living, and then a famine arrives at the perfect moment to be
blamed for a ruin he'd already made. Everyone in this story who fails
wants the same thing: the fruit without the season.

And here is what I can't stop thinking about — she found it, for a while.
Real happiness with someone else. She wasn't lying about that. It simply
had no roots, and a thing with no roots runs out, and by the time it ran
out the thirty years were spent. She didn't seem wired for the slow
version — few of us are, and present things beat future things nearly
every time.
The only reason I'm the exception is that somebody kept knocking while I
still couldn't see the far end of anything. I didn't have a twenty-year
vision of who I'd become. I had Thursdays.

I'll leave the rest of her story where it belongs, which is not on this
page. I don't know how it ends. I hope better than I fear.

## Five

Skip ahead, because I'm allowed to now.

There are grandchildren on the floor. There is a window with trees in it
and light coming through it, which there was not, at the start. There is
a table, a different table, in a room I built to hold it, and a game on
it that I made — Legendary Arena — because making things is the family
trade, and because the truest test of a made thing is what it does to the
people around it. The counterfeits all promise you the world and leave
you alone with it. This one puts people in a room together. That is the
whole reason it exists, and it is the only proof I have of what that year
did to me. A man who was fed, learning at last to feed somebody.

When Elijah was taken, Elisha didn't ask to be his equal. He asked for a
*double portion* — and then he did things his teacher never did. Three
times Elijah told him to stay behind, and three times he refused: *As the
Lord liveth, I will not leave thee.* The student has to insist. Tex never
built an arena. I did. That is not me surpassing the man who saved me —
it is the only way the gift was ever meant to travel.

There is a new man at the table tonight who barely speaks. Lost
something. You can see it on him. Somebody dealt him in without comment,
the way it was done for me, and he'll come back next week not knowing
why, and *in process of time* he won't be able to point to the day it
changed for him either.

I keep a seat for that. It's the most important thing I own.

## What it was for

People ask me, when they hear the shape of it, whether I'd undo the
divorce if I could. I used to think the honest answer was complicated. It
isn't. Looking back from here, it was the greatest blessing of my life —
not because it was good, it was not good, but because it was the door,
and for once I walked through instead of standing in front of it
explaining why I couldn't.

The prodigal had to rehearse a hard sentence on the road home — *Father,
I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, and am no more worthy to
be called thy son.* Not "things went badly." Not "it wasn't my fault."
The father ran to him and cut the speech off before he finished it, but
the son still had to mean every word to get on the road at all. You
cannot be restored to something you won't admit you left. My version was
owning my half of thirty years while she was the one who walked out. It
wasn't fair. It was just the only move that got me anywhere. Blame would
have felt better and cost me the year.

And underneath all of it was the one thing I had been looking for in the
spare room and could not find, because I was looking in the roles. What
cannot be taken from a man is not that he is a husband, or a father, or
an engineer, or even a friend. It is that he is a child of God. It was
true of me on the worst night in that spare room, when I felt like
nothing at all. I hadn't earned it and I couldn't lose it. That is the
*be*, not the *have* — and everything I mistook for my identity had only
ever been fruit hanging off it.

So here is the only thing worth carrying out of that year. I did not end
up with friends because I got lucky. I *became* someone who could be one,
and the friends came after, in that order, and the order is everything.
Take a fisherman's boat and he builds another; take a rich man's money
and he is finished. I am not safe tonight because I have a table full of
people. I am safe because if I lost this one, I know now that I could
build another.

Somebody saw who I could be before I could see it myself. He had the
patience to keep turning up, and I had — eventually, badly, on a Thursday
— the small courage to pick up my keys. That's the offer, and that's the
acceptance. It takes both.

If you're the one in the spare room with your things still in bags, this
is me in the lit doorway. And if you're the one who already made it out —
you know exactly which seat to keep, and who it's for.

The arena awaits. Come sit at the table. You're not alone.

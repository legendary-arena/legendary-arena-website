In [The Door Locks Behind You](/blog/the-door-locks-behind-you/) we set out a
lens for spotting a Scheme without naming a villain. It asks five questions,
in order. Who decides what gets investigated? Who is trusted to report it? Who
controls what may be said? What happens to the person who says no? What does it
cost to leave?

At the table you learn a Scheme by playing against it, not by reading the card.
A lens works the same way. So here it is in use on five ordinary arrangements,
one paragraph each, with no new theory. None of them needs a villain. Each one
has a seat.

## The health plan that comes with the job

Most working Americans get health insurance through their employer. That was
never a plan. It is a leftover from the wage controls of the Second World War,
when employers who could not raise pay competed with benefits instead, and the
tax code later made those benefits tax-free. Run question five and the lock
appears. Leaving the job means leaving the coverage. You can keep it for a while
under COBRA, but at the full price. So people with a sick child, or a condition
of their own, stay in jobs they would otherwise leave. Economists have a name for
it, *job lock*. Nobody guards the door. The paperwork does. The soft lock sits
at question five, and the tell is how much of a working life gets decided by a
benefits form nobody chose.

## The board that licenses its competitors

About one American worker in five now needs a government license to do the job,
up from roughly one in twenty in the 1950s. Run question one: who decides what
counts as qualified? Often it is a board made up of people already in the trade.
In North Carolina, the state dental board, run mostly by practicing dentists,
sent cease-and-desist letters to salons offering cheaper teeth whitening. In
2015 the Supreme Court ruled that a board controlled by active market
participants could not simply claim the state's protection from antitrust law.
The lens does not say licenses are bad. Some protect the public. It asks who set
the scope, and here the incumbents were deciding who gets to compete with them.

## The investigation that reports to the investigated

A police department's internal affairs unit. A university's office reviewing a
complaint against the university. A company's board hiring a law firm to review
the company. A church looking into its own leaders. The uniforms differ; the
structure is the same. Run questions one and two. The body under examination
chooses the investigators, pays them, sets the scope, and decides what gets
released. Sometimes the findings are honest. The lens cannot tell you whether
they are, and that is the problem. The test is whether the inquiry had power to
compel testimony, staff who did not answer to the institution, and a duty to
publish what it found. Without those three, it is a report, not an
investigation.

## The platform that owns your audience

A few platforms carry much of the public conversation. Run question three. The
platform writes the rules for what may be said, enforces them, and hears the
appeals, and people across the political spectrum have at one time or another
found themselves on the losing end. Then run question five. You can usually
download your posts. You cannot take your audience. The followers you built over
years stay behind if you leave. That is the soft lock, and it is what gives the
rules their force: the cost of breaking them is not a fine, it is starting over
from zero. The seat here is not any one executive. It is whoever controls the
rules and the exit at once.

## The loan you cannot walk away from

Almost any debt can be discharged in bankruptcy. Student loans are the notable
exception. Congress began restricting their discharge in 1976, removed the
last time limit in 1998, and extended the rule to private student loans in
2005. They now survive bankruptcy unless the borrower proves "undue hardship,"
a bar courts set high. Run question five and the lock is plain. Now run the questions
in the other direction and ask who carries the risk. The school is paid up
front. The lender is protected by the bankruptcy rule. The eighteen-year-old
signing the note carries nearly all of it. The diagnostic from the last essay
still applies: watch who resists proposals that would make schools share the
loss when their loans go unpaid.

## Ten more, from the news

The five above are slow arrangements, built over decades. The lens works just as
well on events that happened in a few weeks. Here are ten, one from 1973 and
nine from recent years, one short paragraph each, with the question that does the
work. The full version of
each, with sources, is in
[The Door Locks Behind You](/blog/the-door-locks-behind-you/#recent-cases).

**A match that became a picture.** In 1973, before a commonly cited 90 million
viewers, Billie Jean King beat Bobby Riggs, a 55-year-old former champion. The
broadcast left a picture: no difference between men and women. Riggs had beaten
one of the world's top two women, Margaret Court, four months earlier. In 1992
Jimmy Connors beat Martina Navratilova 7–5, 6–2 while playing with one serve
against her wider court. And in 1998 a man ranked 203rd beat both teenage
Williams sisters in a set each. None of those results became the picture. Run question three: nothing was hidden, it just wasn't
chosen.

**A paper that closed a question.** In March 2020 a group of virologists
published a paper in *Nature Medicine* saying no laboratory scenario was
"plausible." Their private messages, released in 2023, show they took a lab
origin far more seriously than the paper did. Run question two: whose seal ended
the argument? A top journal's, which no blog could match.

**A letter from former spies.** Days before the 2020 election, fifty-one former
intelligence officials said the Hunter Biden laptop story looked like Russian
disinformation, while admitting they had no evidence it was. The man who
organized the letter later testified he wanted to help the campaign win. Run
question two again: the title did the work, not the evidence.

**A bank app as a door.** In 2022 Canada let banks freeze the accounts of
convoy protesters without a court order. Two courts later ruled it unlawful.
Run question five: leaving the protest was not argued, it was priced.

**A price on objecting.** At Penn in 2022, swimmers who objected to a
transgender teammate, Lia Thomas, who had swum three seasons on the men's team
as Will Thomas, did it anonymously, for fear of what it would cost them.
Nobody banned speaking up. Run question four: what happens to the person who says
no under their own name?

**An investigation by the defense.** At San José State, the university told its
volleyball coaches not to tell the players that a teammate, Blaire Fleming
(reported by some outlets as born Brayden), was transgender, and
cited privacy law when asked, so the women sharing rooms on road trips were the
last to know. In 2024, when an allegation surfaced involving the team, the
Mountain West investigated through the same law firm defending it in the
players' lawsuit and found "insufficient evidence." The associate head coach
who filed a complaint was pulled from the team four days later and not renewed;
the head coach, who faced a complaint too, stayed. Five schools refused to play, and
each refusal was booked as a loss in the standings. When the players asked a
court to keep Fleming out of the tournament, a judge denied them, and the
tournament was played before any court heard the merits. Run question one: who chose
the investigator? Then question four: nobody banned objecting, but the standings
and the coach's job both charged for it.

**A rule with no definition.** The WNBA's 2026 labor agreement limits the
league to "players who are women" but never defines the word. When two former
NBA players declared for the draft as a protest, the league had its own
executives look into it, announced there was no eligibility question, and
didn't say what standard it used. Run question three: a rule that is never
defined can't be tested by anyone, whether the protesters or the fans who have
speculated for years about Brittney Griner. State the standard, publish the
procedure, allow independent verification.

**A claim nobody made.** A player said she wanted to protect girls in the
locker room from competing against biological men, and said she had never
claimed to hate anyone. A coach answered that the phrasing carried an
"innuendo" that transgender women are predators, and later said, "They don't
want transgender people to exist." Run question three: the claim that was made
never got answered. It got replaced.

**A trade with one box.** In 2022 the United States traded Viktor Bout, the
arms dealer called the "Merchant of Death," for Brittney Griner, a
two-time Defensive Player of the Year with a 2015 disorderly-conduct plea after
a fight with a fiancée, and a later attempt by Griner to annul the 28-day
marriage the day after a twin pregnancy was announced (a court refused, and
ordered child support the annulment would have erased), and left Paul
Whelan, a Marine veteran held since 2018, in a Russian prison. The official
explanation was that the choice was one American or none. Run question one: who
set that menu? Whelan waited until 2024.

**A broadcast that picked the event.** In 2017 stadium crowds booed kneeling
players while television made the kneel the national picture, then stopped
showing the anthem at all. Run question three: two events happened that Sunday,
and only one of them was allowed to be *the* event.

## Now run it on something you love

Fifteen arrangements, and not one of them needed a villain. Each is held in place
by a seat, a scope, or a door that is expensive to walk back through.

It would be easy to stop here, with five examples comfortably outside your own
life. Don't. The lens only works if you point it at everything, including your
employer, your party, your church, your club, and the game you play on Friday
night. Ask the five questions of the arrangement you would most like to defend.
If the answers are clean, you will know your loyalty is well placed, and you will
know it for a reason. If they are not, better to find that out from five honest
questions than from the door.

At the table, the heroes who win are the ones who read the Scheme early. The same
habit works anywhere. Check the door before you need it.

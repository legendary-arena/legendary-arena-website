There are innocents in the villain deck.

They're called **bystanders** — ordinary people, no powers, caught in the wrong
place when the villains came through. A villain enters the city carrying one:
captured, being dragged toward the exit. Defeat that villain in time and you
pull the bystander free — rescued, safe, added to the people you got out.
Let that villain reach the end of the city and escape, and it takes the
bystander with it, off the edge of the board and gone.

Here's the quiet, dangerous thing about them: **the game does not force you to
care.** You can win — stop the scheme, defeat the mastermind, end the game on
the right side of it — with a whole line of villains having slipped past you
into the dark, each one carrying someone you were supposed to save. The
scoreboard will still say you won. It just won't mention the city you emptied
to do it.

## The flashier target

Every turn offers you something impressive to do. A big villain to knock down.
A hero to recruit. An engine to build one card taller. The bystander being
carried toward the exit is never the flashiest play on the board — it's a
smaller villain, off to the side, easy to leave for next turn.

And next turn it's gone.

This is how good players lose the thing that mattered while winning everything
that's measured. **You get busy being effective.** You chase the target that
builds your position, you optimize the turn, you rack up the defeats that feel
like progress — and the whole time, off at the edge of your attention, the
people are being carried out one at a time by threats you decided weren't worth
turning for. Nothing about that feels like failure in the moment. It feels like
focus.

It's the oldest trap there is, and the sharpest warning ever put on it is a
question: **"what is a man profited, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose
his own soul?"** (Matthew 16:26). Gain the whole board. Lose the point of
playing on it.

## Winning the fight is not saving the people

We keep these two things separate everywhere except in our own lives, where we
constantly confuse them.

The parent who provides everything and is present for none of it won the fight
— the bills, the house, the career — and let the years escape carrying the
kids. The believer who wins every argument about the faith and loses the person
they were arguing with. The leader who hits every number while the people
delivering them quietly walk out the exit. In every case the scoreboard is
clean and the city is empty, and the scoreboard was never what you were
actually protecting. **It was standing in for the people the whole time.**

That's the discipline the bystander teaches, turn after turn: notice which of
your wins are the real thing and which are proxies for it. Defeating the
villain is a proxy. Building the engine is a proxy. The person the villain was
carrying is not a proxy for anything. They're the reason the fight exists.

## The one

Look closer and the point gets sharper, because bystanders don't arrive as a
statistic. They arrive one at a time. This villain is carrying *this* person.
The choice to turn and rescue is never about a category; it's about the single
figure being dragged toward the edge right now.

Scripture keeps insisting on exactly this scale. The shepherd leaves the
ninety and nine and goes after the *one*. "Remember," the revelation says,
"the worth of souls is great in the sight of God" (D&C 18:10) — souls, plural,
but the worth is counted one at a time, because that's the only way worth like
that can be counted. **A person is never a data point.** The moment you start
thinking in totals — acceptable losses, most of them made it, the numbers are
fine — you've already stopped seeing the one being carried out, and the one is
the whole game.

## The turn it costs you

Rescue is rarely free. The bystander is being carried by a villain you'd
otherwise ignore, in a direction away from your best play, and turning to catch
it means *not* doing the efficient, board-building thing this turn. It costs you
tempo. It costs you the impressive move. On paper, stopping to save one person
is almost always the worse play.

Make it anyway. **The turn you spend turning back is not a tax on the mission —
it is the mission.** The efficient line that leaves someone behind didn't win
more; it just kept better books while losing the thing the books were for. If
your whole game is optimized to the point where you can't afford to turn for the
one being carried off, you have optimized away the reason you were playing.

## What the whole thing is for

When the game ends, you have two tallies that the final score blurs together:
the people you got out, and the ones who were carried past you while you were
busy. That second number doesn't show up as a loss. It shows up as an empty
place in the city that nobody scores and you never quite forget.

Keep it in view from the first turn. Not because it's on the scoreboard —
because it's the point the scoreboard is a stand-in for. The reason any of it
exists is stated as plainly as anything ever gets stated: **"this is my work
and my glory — to bring to pass the immortality and eternal life of man"**
(Moses 1:39). The whole enterprise, from the beginning, was people.

So win the fight. Beat the mastermind, stop the scheme, build the engine — all
of it. But do not let the villains walk out of the city carrying the ones you
came to save, and then call it a victory because the number was good.

You don't remember the score afterward. You remember who you got out.

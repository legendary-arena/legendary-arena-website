Every deckbuilder has a collection of habits that feel right and play wrong. These traps are persistent because they are intuitive — buying powerful cards feels productive, keeping options open feels safe, and spending all your resources feels efficient. But intuition optimizes for comfort, not performance. Here are the ten traps, what makes each one seductive, and how to break free.

## Trap 1: The Buffet

**What it looks like:** Buying every attractive card in the recruitment row regardless of synergy. Your deck has something from every faction, every cost tier, and every strategy — and does none of them well.

**Why it happens:** Each card looks good in isolation. A strong attacker here, a good economy card there. They are all fine cards. But fine cards without a unifying strategy produce a deck that draws random handfuls of unrelated abilities.

**The fix:** Apply your [strategy sentence](/blog/week-01-deck-checklist/) to every purchase. If a card does not serve the sentence, it does not enter your deck — no matter how good it looks alone.

## Trap 2: Ignoring the Curve

**What it looks like:** A deck loaded with expensive cards and nothing to play in the first four turns. You sit there with a hand full of recruit-generating starters while your engine sits at the bottom of the deck.

**Why it happens:** Big cards are exciting. Small cards feel incremental. So you buy big and hope your early draws carry you.

**The fix:** Map your [resource curve](/blog/week-02-resource-curve/) after every recruit. If your mid-range is empty, fill it before adding another finisher.

## Trap 3: No Thinning

**What it looks like:** A forty-card deck that still contains every starter card from turn one. Your best cards appear once every eight turns instead of every five.

**Why it happens:** Thinning feels like losing a card. It is counterintuitive to pay resources to remove something from your deck when you could spend those resources to add something.

**The fix:** Think of thinning as increasing the concentration of your good cards. Every weak card removed is a draw step that now hits something useful.

## Trap 4: Over-Specializing

**What it looks like:** A deck that does one thing brilliantly and literally cannot handle anything else. When the game asks for flexibility, you have none.

**Why it happens:** Specialization feels like commitment to a plan. And it is — until the plan meets a Scenario that punishes it.

**The fix:** Keep your core strategy focused, but leave two to three [flex slots](/blog/week-28-flex-slots/) for adaptability. Specialization to ninety percent with ten percent flexibility outperforms one hundred percent specialization.

## Trap 5: Under-Specializing

**What it looks like:** A deck that tries to cover every role — attack, economy, draw, defense, thinning — and does none of them at a level that matters.

**Why it happens:** Fear of being caught without an answer. If you have a little bit of everything, nothing can surprise you. But nothing can threaten the Scenario either.

**The fix:** Commit to a primary role and a secondary role. Accept that some things will be outside your capability and rely on teammates or smart play to compensate.

## Trap 6: Ignoring the Scenario

**What it looks like:** Building the same deck regardless of what the Scenario demands. Your pet strategy goes in every game, and when it does not work, you blame luck.

**Why it happens:** Familiarity is comfortable. Building a new strategy every game is mentally expensive.

**The fix:** [Scout the Scenario](/blog/week-03-scenario-scouting/) before you recruit your first card. Let the Scenario inform your strategy sentence, not the other way around.

## Trap 7: Hoarding Resources

**What it looks like:** Ending turns with unspent recruit points or uncommitted attack. "I am saving for something better." Meanwhile, the Scenario does not wait.

**Why it happens:** Leaving resources uncommitted feels like keeping options open. It is actually just wasting them.

**The fix:** Unspent resources at end of turn are lost. If nothing in the recruitment row is a [keep or buy](/blog/week-06-hand-evaluation/), spend on thinning. If no thinning is available, accept the loss — but do not pretend hoarding is a strategy.

## Trap 8: Panic Recruiting

**What it looks like:** A sudden burst of unplanned purchases when a big threat appears. The Scenario escalates, you feel behind, and you start buying anything that looks like it might help — economy, attack, utility, all in one turn.

**Why it happens:** Stress overrides discipline. When you feel threatened, the impulse is to do something, and buying cards feels like action.

**The fix:** Panic purchases dilute your deck exactly when you need it to be sharp. When a crisis hits, pause. Check whether your existing deck can handle it before adding cards that will not appear in your hand for several turns.

## Trap 9: Neglecting Utility

**What it looks like:** A deck stacked with attackers and economy cards but no draw, no filtering, no thinning. Raw power without the engine to deliver it consistently.

**Why it happens:** Utility effects do not feel like progress. Drawing extra cards or filtering your deck does not defeat Villains directly, so it feels like a detour.

**The fix:** Utility is the engine that makes your power cards appear when you need them. One draw-two card generates more total value over a game than one additional attacker because it accelerates your entire deck.

## Trap 10: Failing to Adapt

**What it looks like:** Executing the same game plan turn after turn even when the board is screaming that it is not working. Staying the course is stubbornness, not strategy.

**Why it happens:** Pivoting feels like admitting a mistake. Staying feels like commitment.

**The fix:** Revisit your strategy sentence every few turns. If the game state no longer matches the assumptions behind that sentence, [pivot](/blog/week-09-mid-game-pivot/). Adapting is not failure — refusing to adapt is.

## Try this

Pick one trap from this list — the one you suspect you fall into most often — and ban it for your next three games. Actively watch for the moment it tempts you, and consciously choose the alternative. One trap eliminated per week compounds into a dramatically better player over a season.

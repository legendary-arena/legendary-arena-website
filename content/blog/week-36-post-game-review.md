Most post-game reviews fail because they try to review everything. You replay the entire game in your head, note a dozen things that went right or wrong, and leave with a vague sense that you should "play better." That is not a review. That is a memory exercise.

Effective review has one job: find the single decision that most changed the outcome, determine whether it was correct, and extract one actionable rule for next time.

## The one-decision method

After every game — win or lose — ask one question: what was the single decision that most affected the outcome?

Not the most dramatic moment. Not the biggest attack turn. The decision point where the game's trajectory shifted. Sometimes it is obvious — a pivotal recruit, a critical attack allocation. Sometimes it is subtle — the moment you decided not to thin, the turn you kept building instead of closing.

Finding this decision requires honesty. In wins, the temptation is to credit your best play. In losses, the temptation is to blame luck. The one-decision method strips away ego and asks: where did the game turn?

## Evaluating the decision

Once identified, evaluate:

**Was it correct?** Given what you knew at the time (not what you know now), was it the best available choice?

If yes: what made it correct? Identify the reasoning so you can replicate it. Correct decisions that feel automatic are skills. Correct decisions that required deliberation are frameworks you need to practice until they become automatic.

If no: what would have been better? Identify the alternative specifically — not "I should have played better" but "I should have recruited the draw card instead of the attacker because my engine needed fuel more than payload."

## The "next time" rule

From the evaluation, write one rule. One sentence. Specific and actionable.

**Good rules:**
- "Prioritize draw effects over attack in the first three recruits when playing engine."
- "Check the closing window every turn after mid-game instead of waiting until the board forces it."
- "Thin starter cards before turn five whenever thinning is available."

**Bad rules:**
- "Play better." (Not actionable.)
- "Don't lose." (Not specific.)
- "Consider more options." (Not concrete.)

The rule should be something you can consciously apply in your next game. Over weeks and months, your rules accumulate into a personal playbook — a set of lessons extracted from your own experience.

## The personal playbook

Keep your rules in one place — a notebook, a document, a notes app. Review them before playing. Over time, you will notice patterns: the same types of mistakes reappearing, the same types of decisions being evaluated. Those patterns are your skill gaps.

When a rule appears three times, it is not a one-off mistake. It is a habit. That habit needs dedicated [practice](/blog/week-34-practice/).

When a rule stops appearing, the lesson has been internalized. Remove it from active review (keep it in the archive) and focus on newer rules.

## Review in wins and losses

Review losses for mistakes: where did you go wrong?

Review wins for missed opportunities: where could you have played better even though you won? Winning games often contain errors that were masked by the outcome. A win where you threw your lead and got lucky to recover is a loss in disguise — your review should treat it as one.

The discipline of reviewing wins is harder than reviewing losses, but it is what separates players who plateau from players who keep improving.

## Try this

After your next three games, apply the one-decision method. Find the decision, evaluate it, write the rule. After three games, review your three rules. Do they share a theme? If so, that theme is your current growth edge.

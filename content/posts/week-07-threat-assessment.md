The board is full of threats. Villains are stacking up, the Scheme is advancing, and your hand has enough attack to deal with maybe one of them. Which one do you hit?

This is where most players default to instinct — they attack the biggest number, or the Villain closest to escaping, or the one that annoys them most. Instinct is fast, but it is not a strategy. The danger ladder gives you a repeatable method for ranking threats so you spend your limited resources where they matter most.

## The three tiers of threat

Not all threats are equally dangerous. They fall into three categories, and the categories determine your priority.

### Tier 1 — Immediate threats

An immediate threat is one that will cause irreversible damage if you do not address it this turn. A Villain about to escape with a devastating effect. A Scheme twist threshold about to trigger. A board state that, if left alone for one more turn, puts the game out of reach.

Immediate threats get your attack first, even if dealing with them feels inefficient. Efficiency does not matter when the game ends next turn.

### Tier 2 — Scaling threats

A scaling threat is one that grows worse over time but is not immediately lethal. A Villain that generates additional Villains each turn. A board condition that stacks incremental penalties. A Scheme that escalates gradually.

Scaling threats are the most dangerous to ignore because they compound. A scaling threat left alone for three turns might require more total resources to deal with than if you had handled it immediately. The correct play is often to address scaling threats before they cross the threshold into Tier 1.

### Tier 3 — Passive threats

A passive threat occupies space and applies a static penalty but does not escalate. A Villain with a persistent debuff. A board condition that taxes you each turn without growing. These are annoying, but they do not spiral.

Passive threats get your attack last. If you have surplus damage after handling Tiers 1 and 2, clean them up. If not, live with them. A passive tax on your economy is tolerable. An unaddressed scaling threat is not.

## The ladder in practice

Each turn, before committing attack, run through the ladder:

1. **Scan for Tier 1.** Is anything about to cause irreversible damage? If yes, commit whatever resources are needed to stop it. If no, move on.
2. **Scan for Tier 2.** Is anything scaling that will become Tier 1 within two turns? If yes, calculate whether you can address it now or must plan over multiple turns. Address it if possible.
3. **Assess Tier 3.** Are passive threats worth cleaning up this turn, or is your attack better spent elsewhere? Often the answer is elsewhere.

This scan takes seconds once you practice it. It replaces gut-feel targeting with a structured read.

## When to sacrifice short-term advantage

Sometimes the correct play is to take a hit now to prevent a worse hit later. A Tier 2 threat that you could ignore this turn — saving your attack for a Tier 3 cleanup — might become a Tier 1 emergency next turn, costing you far more to handle.

The ladder forces you to think one turn ahead. If today's comfortable play creates tomorrow's crisis, it is not actually comfortable. Spend your resources on prevention when prevention is cheaper than reaction.

## The trap of overkill

A common mistake is committing more attack than necessary to a single threat. If a Villain requires five attack to defeat and you have eight, spending all eight on that Villain wastes three points. Those three points could have dented a Tier 2 threat, thinned a Villain stack, or set up next turn.

Check the math before committing. Hit the threshold, then redirect surplus.

But there is a counter-trap: splitting damage across two targets and defeating neither. Three attack into a five-health Villain and three into a four-health Villain leaves both alive and your damage wasted. If you cannot defeat a target, do not invest in it unless chip damage has a mechanical benefit (some effects trigger on any damage dealt, not just lethal).

## Reading the board as a team

In co-op play, threat assessment is a shared responsibility. Communicate the ladder. If you see a Tier 1 threat and your partner sees a Tier 2, coordinate: one player handles the emergency, the other invests in the scaling threat. Without communication, both players might pile onto the same target while something else spirals.

Quick shorthand at the start of each round: "I see a Tier 1 at [position]. Can you handle Tier 2?" That sentence costs five seconds and prevents cascading failures.

## Try this

Next game, before your first attack each turn, verbally rank every threat on the board: Tier 1, 2, or 3. Then commit your attack in order. After the game, identify the turn where threat prioritization most affected the outcome — positively or negatively.

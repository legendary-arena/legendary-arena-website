Your deck has twenty-five cards. Fifteen of them are excellent — exactly what you need. The other ten are "pretty good." They contribute something, but they are not core to your strategy. Every time you draw one of those ten, you are not drawing one of the fifteen that actually matters.

That is dilution. It is not the obviously bad cards that kill your deck — you already know to skip those. It is the good-enough cards, the reasonable purchases, the ones that individually make sense but collectively drag your deck away from precision toward mediocrity.

## Why dilution happens

Dilution happens because deckbuilding games reward you for buying cards. Every turn, you have recruit points. Every turn, the recruitment row offers something. Spending resources feels productive. Holding back feels wasteful. So you buy a card that is fine, then another that is fine, and your deck grows from a focused twenty-five to a bloated thirty-five, and you wonder why your engine stopped feeling sharp.

The hidden cost of every card added to your deck: every other card in your deck becomes slightly harder to draw. Your best card in a twenty-five-card deck appears once every five turns. In a thirty-five-card deck, once every seven turns. Same card, same ability, thirty percent less frequent. That is the tax of dilution.

## Anti-synergy: when good cards fight each other

Dilution is not just about deck size. It is about cards that conflict.

A card that rewards you for playing many cards in a turn and a card that rewards you for having a small hand size are both individually strong. Together, they work against each other. Playing many cards empties your hand, disabling the hand-size bonus. Keeping a full hand prevents you from triggering the many-cards bonus.

Anti-synergy is dilution's cousin. Your deck is not just bigger — it is fighting itself. Cards that are individually rated eight out of ten can produce a deck that performs at five.

## The discipline of skipping

The most powerful deckbuilding skill is skipping a good buy. Not skipping a bad buy — anyone can do that. Skipping a good buy because your deck does not need it.

The test: if this card replaced the weakest card in your current deck, would it improve your worst-case turn? If no — if it just adds another decent option without removing a weak one — then buying it makes your deck bigger without making it better.

A lean deck of twenty-five focused cards outperforms a bloated deck of thirty-five good cards because the lean deck draws its best cards more often, fires its synergies more consistently, and cycles through its engine faster.

## Deck size as a hidden variable

Most players do not track their deck size. They know which cards they bought but not how many total cards they have. Start tracking.

After your fifth recruit, count your deck. If you are past thirty and your strategy depends on seeing specific cards together, you are at risk of dilution. Every recruit from this point forward should either replace a card (through thinning) or be so powerful that the dilution cost is worth it.

A useful heuristic: if you would not be happy to see the card in every one of your next three hands, do not add it to your deck.

## When bigger is better

Dilution is the default concern, but there are games where a larger deck is acceptable:

- If your draw effects are powerful enough to see most of your deck every cycle, size matters less.
- If the game rewards breadth (answering diverse threats with diverse tools), a wider deck is intentional, not diluted.
- If thinning is unavailable and the only way to improve your deck is to add better cards on top of bad ones, growth is the only option.

These are exceptions. The default is lean.

## Try this

Before your next game, set a deck-size target. "I will not exceed thirty cards." Track every recruit and note which purchases were strategic (filling a role, supporting synergy) versus habitual (spending recruit because it was available). After the game, identify which purchases were dilution in disguise.

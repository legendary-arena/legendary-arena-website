A deck that averages seven attack per turn and never drops below five will beat a deck that averages seven but swings between two and twelve. Same average. Different outcomes. The consistent deck wins because deckbuilding games do not reward average performance — they punish bad turns. One turn at two attack when you needed five means a Villain escapes, a Scheme advances, or a crisis goes unanswered. The three turns at twelve cannot undo that damage.

Consistency is not about lowering your ceiling. It is about raising your floor.

## The consistency spectrum

Every deck sits on a spectrum from volatile to consistent:

**Volatile decks** have high variance. They produce spectacular turns and terrible turns in unpredictable patterns. Volatile decks feel exciting — the highs are thrilling. But the lows cost games.

**Consistent decks** have low variance. They produce reliable turns within a narrow range. Consistent decks feel boring — no explosive moments. But they rarely have turns where they cannot respond to the board.

The goal is not to eliminate all variance. It is to compress the range so that your worst turns are still functional.

## Three tools for consistency

### Redundancy

Redundancy means having multiple cards that fill the same role. If your strategy requires draw effects, do not rely on a single draw card. Recruit three. Any hand that contains at least one of them activates your engine.

The math: if your deck has one copy of a critical card in thirty cards, you have roughly a 17% chance of seeing it in any five-card hand. With three copies, that climbs to roughly 42%. Redundancy nearly triples the probability of your engine activating each turn.

Do not confuse redundancy with duplication. You do not need three identical cards. You need three cards that serve the same function. A draw-two, a draw-one-plus-filter, and a look-at-three-keep-two all provide card access. Different cards, same role, same redundancy benefit.

### Thinning

Thinning removes weak cards from your deck, increasing the concentration of strong cards. Every starter card removed is a future draw that now hits something useful instead of something dead.

Thinning is the single most undervalued tool in deckbuilding. New players resist it because it feels like losing a card. But a thirty-card deck with five dead cards draws a dead card once every six hands. A twenty-five-card deck with zero dead cards never draws dead. That difference is the difference between consistent and volatile.

### Filtering

Filtering lets you look at multiple cards and choose the best one. Effects that say "look at the top three cards, add one to your hand, discard the rest" are filtering. They do not increase your hand size, but they increase hand quality.

Filtering smooths variance by letting you skip the worst options in your deck. It pairs especially well with redundancy — the more good cards in your deck, the better your filtered selections.

## Building consistently: the practical approach

1. **Set a floor.** Define the minimum turn your deck must produce. "At least four attack every turn." Then build to guarantee that floor before chasing higher ceilings.
2. **Recruit for redundancy before power.** A second draw effect contributes more to your win rate than a flashy finisher because it ensures your engine fires consistently.
3. **Thin early and aggressively.** Every thinning effect recruited in the first five turns pays dividends for the rest of the game.
4. **Avoid single points of failure.** If your strategy collapses when one specific card is at the bottom of your deck, your strategy is inconsistent. Redesign it with redundant paths.

## Try this

After your next game, review your turn-by-turn output. Note your best turn and your worst turn. Calculate the gap. If the gap is larger than double your average, your deck is too volatile. Identify one change — more redundancy, more thinning, or more filtering — that would have compressed the range.

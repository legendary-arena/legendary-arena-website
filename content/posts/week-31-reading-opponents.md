In multiplayer deckbuilding, every card in the shared recruitment row is contested. What your opponent buys changes what is available to you. What they build determines the threats they can handle — and the ones they leave for you. Reading your opponent's intentions is not about mind games. It is about information that is already visible if you know where to look.

Two signals reliably predict what an opponent will buy next.

## Signal 1: Trajectory

Trajectory is the pattern of an opponent's previous purchases. If they have bought three attack-focused cards in a row, they will likely buy a fourth. If their last two purchases were economy cards, they are building an engine. Trajectory tells you what strategy they are committed to.

**How to read it:** After each of your opponent's turns, note what they recruited. After three to four recruits, the trajectory is usually clear: aggressive, engine, control, or hybrid.

**How to use it:** Once you know their trajectory, you can predict which cards in the row they will target. If they are building attack, the next strong attacker in the row is their likely target. Plan your own recruitment around this — either grab it first if you need it, or let them have it and focus on cards that complement your own strategy.

**Limitation:** Trajectory assumes they stay the course. A skilled opponent may [pivot](/posts/week-09-mid-game-pivot/) mid-game, and you need to detect the pivot when it happens.

## Signal 2: Gap analysis

Gap analysis looks at what an opponent's deck is missing. Every deck has strengths and weaknesses. If an opponent has heavy attack but no draw, they need draw effects to reach consistency. If they have strong economy but no finishers, they will soon recruit something expensive.

**How to read it:** Look at what their deck does well (visible from their play each turn) and ask: what is the gap? What would they need to buy to round out their build?

**How to use it:** The gap tells you what they will want even if it breaks their trajectory. A player on an attack trajectory who suddenly has a draw card available in the row will likely grab it because draw is their biggest gap. You can predict the deviance from trajectory by knowing the gap.

## Combining the signals

Trajectory tells you where they are going. Gap analysis tells you what they need to get there. Together, they narrow the field to one or two cards in the row that your opponent is most likely to target.

**Example:** Opponent trajectory is engine-building (economy + draw). Their gap is a finisher — they have the engine but no payload. A seven-cost attacker appears in the row. Even though it breaks their economy-card trajectory, their gap analysis predicts they will shift to grab the finisher. If you also need a finisher, you now know to recruit it before their turn.

## When reads inform hate-drafting

If your read tells you that a specific card will significantly improve your opponent's deck, and that card is the difference between their strategy working and failing, a surgical [hate-draft](/posts/week-22-contested-drafting/) may be correct. Buy the card they need, deny the upgrade, and accept the minor dilution to your own deck.

But only if the read is confident. A bad hate-draft wastes your recruit on a card you cannot use while your opponent finds an alternative anyway.

## Keeping your own signals ambiguous

If you know to read opponents, they know to read you. Two techniques for ambiguity:

**Neutral early buys.** Economy and draw cards serve any strategy. Buying them early delays the point at which your trajectory becomes readable.

**Disguised pivots.** If you plan to pivot from economy to aggression mid-game, the pivot appears as a trajectory change that opponents may misread as desperation rather than intention.

Ambiguity has costs — delaying your core build to stay hidden slows your engine. Use it in competitive settings where information asymmetry matters. Skip it in casual play.

## Try this

In your next multiplayer game, track one opponent's trajectory by noting their first four purchases. After purchase four, predict their fifth purchase before their turn. Were you right? If not, check whether the gap analysis would have predicted the deviation.

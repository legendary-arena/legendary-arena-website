You are behind. The Scheme is advancing, Villains are stacking up, and your deck cannot keep pace. The instinct is to fight harder — throw everything at the biggest threat and hope it turns the game around. That instinct is wrong. Fighting harder when you are losing accelerates the loss because you are spending resources on offense when your position is collapsing.

The comeback blueprint has three phases, and the first one is not attacking. It is stabilizing.

## The stabilization threshold

Stabilization is the point where the Scenario's pressure stops exceeding your capacity to respond. Below the threshold, you are losing ground every turn — threats accumulate faster than you clear them. At the threshold, you are treading water — matching the Scenario's pace without falling further behind. Above it, you are gaining ground.

Your first goal when behind is to reach the threshold. Not to win. Not to catch up. Just to stop the slide.

## Phase 1: Stop the bleeding

Identify the single largest source of damage to your position. It might be a Villain with a recurring penalty, a Scheme mechanism that punishes you each turn, or a snowballing board state. Whatever it is, that is your sole target.

**Trade damage for safety.** You may need to sacrifice offensive potential to address the bleeding. Skip a strong recruit to buy a defensive card instead. Redirect attack from a valuable Villain to an urgent one. The math of stabilization is trading optimal plays for survival plays.

**Do not panic recruit.** When behind, the temptation is to buy anything that looks helpful. This is [Trap 8](/posts/week-11-beginner-traps/). Panic purchases dilute your deck at the worst possible time. If a card does not directly help you stabilize, skip it.

**Communicate the crisis.** In co-op, tell your team: "I need help stabilizing." A teammate redirecting one attack to cover your position is often cheaper than you trying to solo the recovery.

## Phase 2: Rebuild

Once the bleeding stops and you are matching the Scenario's pace, shift to rebuilding. This phase is about closing the gap between where you are and where you need to be.

**Assess what is missing.** Why were you behind? Was it a bad curve, a missed pivot, insufficient draw? Identify the structural problem and recruit to fix it — but only one or two targeted purchases, not a full deck overhaul.

**Thin aggressively.** A deck that fell behind is often bloated with early-game cards that no longer contribute. If thinning effects are available, this is the best time to use them. Every dead card removed increases the probability that your remaining draws are useful.

**Play for efficiency, not power.** During rebuild, your turns should be clean and consistent — not spectacular. Consistent sixes beat inconsistent tens because you cannot afford another bad turn.

## Phase 3: Strike

When your position is stable and your deck is functional again, shift to offense. The strike phase is a compressed version of the [closing sequence](/posts/week-10-closing/) — you have less time and fewer resources than a player who led all game, so you must be more precise.

**Pick the tightest path to victory.** No side quests, no optional threats, no "nice to have" recruits. What is the minimum sequence of turns that gets you to a win?

**Accept imperfection.** A comeback win is rarely clean. You will close with a thinner margin, less backup, and more risk than a front-runner. That is fine. A messy win beats a tidy loss.

## When the comeback is not available

Not every behind-state is recoverable. If the Scenario has escalated past your deck's theoretical maximum output, stabilization is impossible and the game is lost. Recognizing this early saves time and emotional energy. Play out the remaining turns for practice value — test ideas, try risky lines, learn from the loss — but do not pretend you are still competing.

## Try this

Next time you fall behind in a game, resist the urge to immediately fight back. Instead, identify the stabilization threshold: what needs to happen for the bleeding to stop? Focus your entire next turn on reaching that threshold and nothing else. After the game, evaluate whether early stabilization changed the trajectory.

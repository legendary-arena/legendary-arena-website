Speed and quality are not opposites. The fastest players in competitive deckbuilding are often the most accurate, because speed comes from preparation and pattern recognition, not from rushing. A player who agonizes over every decision is not being thorough — they are processing in real time what should have been processed in advance.

## Why slow play is often bad play

Analysis paralysis — the state of deliberating so long that no decision feels certain — is not careful thinking. It is the absence of a decision framework. A player with a clear strategy and practiced execution makes decisions in seconds because the framework tells them what to do. A player without a framework stares at the board and weighs every option from scratch each turn.

Slow play also creates a secondary problem: fatigue. In a tournament, decisions pile up over multiple rounds. If each decision costs significant mental energy because it is computed from scratch, you arrive at late rounds depleted. Fast, framework-driven decisions conserve energy for the moments that actually require deep thought.

## Pre-planning during downtime

In multiplayer games, your opponents' turns are not idle time. They are planning time.

**During opponents' turns, pre-plan your next turn.** Assess the board. Identify your likely targets. Sketch your play order. By the time your turn starts, you should know your first three actions. Only adjust if the board state changed during opponents' turns in a way that invalidates your plan.

**Between rounds in tournaments, prepare for the next Scenario.** Review your [matchup plan](/posts/week-27-matchups/). Check your flex slot options. Reset your mental state. The between-round gap is for preparation, not recovery.

## Decision trees: narrow choices before your turn

A decision tree is a pre-built "if this, then that" structure for common situations:

**Recruitment decision tree:**
- Is a keep card available and affordable? → Buy it.
- No keep card? Is a flex-slot gap fillable? → Fill it.
- Neither? → Thin or skip.

**Attack decision tree:**
- Is a Tier 1 threat active? → Address it.
- No Tier 1? → Address the highest Tier 2 threat.
- No Tier 2? → Clean up Tier 3 or bank attack.

**Mid-game decision tree:**
- Is my engine online? → Start converting.
- Not yet? → Continue building.
- Engine online but game is still early? → Build one more turn, then convert.

These trees handle eighty percent of decisions instantly. The remaining twenty percent — the genuinely novel situations — deserve your full attention. Decision trees free up mental bandwidth for the decisions that actually matter.

## The 60-second rule

Set a personal time limit: sixty seconds per decision point. If you have not decided within sixty seconds, take the safe option. This is not about rushing — it is about recognizing that decisions that take longer than sixty seconds are usually agonized over, not analyzed. The difference between your sixty-second instinct and your five-minute deliberation is rarely large enough to justify the time cost.

Exceptions: first-turn recruitment in a tournament round (take ninety seconds — the stakes are higher) and closing turns (take the time to verify the math).

## Practice drills for speed

**Drill 1: Timed turns.** Set a timer for your turns in practice games. Start at ninety seconds per turn and work down to sixty. Note which decisions slow you down — those are the decisions that need better frameworks.

**Drill 2: Instant read.** When the recruitment row refreshes, immediately classify each card as keep, buy, or skip. Practice until the classification is instant.

**Drill 3: Pre-plan confirmation.** At the start of your turn, check whether your pre-plan (made during opponent's turns) is still valid. If it is, execute without re-deliberating. If it is not, adjust and move.

## Try this

In your next game, time yourself. How long does each turn take? Identify the three longest turns and determine what decision caused the delay. Build a decision tree for that type of decision and use it next game.

## How Fixed-Pool actually works

This is the part most people get wrong, so it's worth being precise.

Fixed-Pool **does not assign you a roster.** There is no pre-set team for this gauntlet, and nothing anywhere publishes "the heroes for Magneto." What it gives you is a **budget**:

> Across every leg of your entry, the union of all heroes you used must fit inside `heroCount + 2`.

| Players | Heroes per match | Fixed-Pool budget | Substitutions across the whole gauntlet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| 2–4 | 5 | 7 | 2 |
| 5 | 6 | 8 | 2 |

That last column is the entire strategic content of the division. **Two.** Whatever the player count, the budget is exactly two heroes wider than a single match. Eight schemes, and you may introduce two heroes beyond your starting five.

The other thing to internalise: **heroes are not restricted to the Core Set.** The gauntlet pins Magneto and the eight schemes; your heroes come from anywhere in your collection. So the budget is not "5 of these 18" — it is five slots spent against everything you own. The Core Set's own eighteen (Black Widow, Captain America, Cyclops, Deadpool, Emma Frost, Gambit, Hawkeye, Hulk, Iron Man, Nick Fury, Rogue, Spider-Man, Storm, Thor, Wolverine, and the three S.H.I.E.L.D. hero cards) are a thematic default, not a fence.

## Why this inverts normal deck selection

In the Open division, the correct play is to counter-pick. The Legacy Virus rewards one kind of build, Super Hero Civil War another, and you are free to bring the right answer each time.

Fixed-Pool removes that freedom, and in doing so it inverts what "good" means:

**Open division optimises the best case. Fixed-Pool optimises the worst case.**

A hero who is devastating against two schemes and inert against the other six is an excellent Open pick and a poor Fixed-Pool core. A hero who is never the best answer but never a dead draw is nearly worthless in Open — you could always have brought something better — and is exactly what wins Fixed-Pool.

The practical selection rule: **for each candidate, ask what your worst leg looks like with them in the pool.** Not the best. Fixed-Pool entries fail on their weakest leg, because a single unwon scheme means no entry at all.

## Spending the two flex slots

The budget's two extra heroes are the only real decision the format offers, and there are two coherent ways to spend them.

**The patch.** Identify your core's single worst matchup among the eight, and bring one hero specifically to fix it. This is the safe play. It converts your weakest leg into a merely-average leg, which is what completeness rewards.

**The pivot.** Use both slots on one scheme that your core genuinely cannot solve, running a substantially different five for that leg. This is higher variance and occasionally correct — but note it costs your whole flex allowance on a single leg, leaving no patch anywhere else.

At solo the maths is harshest: three heroes per match against a five-hero budget. Your core three appear in all eight legs, and you have two cards of slack for the entire championship. Solo Fixed-Pool is the most demanding format on the board and, when the boards fill, likely the most prestigious line on it.

## An order of operations that works

1. **Play the eight legs in Open first.** Learn which schemes punish which weaknesses. This costs you nothing — Open entries are ranked on their own board, so the reconnaissance is itself a ranked run.
2. **Note your two hardest legs.** Not the ones you lost, the ones you barely won.
3. **Build a core that is merely fine against those two**, rather than excellent against the five you already handle.
4. **Spend the flex on the remaining problem.**
5. **Re-run legs freely.** Scoring is best-per-leg, so a failed experiment costs only time. There is no downside to a second attempt.

## On Magneto specifically

Magneto sits at the top of the original Core Set, and the eight schemes beneath him are the ones that defined the game — Midtown Bank Robbery through Unleash the Power of the Cosmic Cube. It is the most recognisable gauntlet on the board, which is part of why it is worth claiming: **a Fixed-Pool Championship here is the most legible achievement in the format.** Everyone knows this set.

It is also eight legs, which means the completeness rule bites. If you want a shorter first Fixed-Pool attempt, the four-leg S.H.I.E.L.D. gauntlet is the gentler introduction to the same constraint — same budget rule, half the exposure.

## What this section will say later

Straight about the limits: **no Fixed-Pool board has been claimed yet, so everything above is reasoning about the format rather than evidence from it.** I can tell you how the budget works and what it does to selection logic, because that is in the rules. I cannot yet tell you which five heroes win it, because nobody knows.

That is exactly what replay verification is for. Once entries land, this section becomes real data — the actual pool compositions of ranked Fixed-Pool entries, per-leg score spreads, and which cores survive all eight schemes. Until then, treat the above as a framework, not an answer.

Every Fixed-Pool board is currently unclaimed. When the boards open, the first person to solve this one gets their pool published next to their name.

> **One honest caveat before you start.** The gauntlet boards are not accepting entries yet. Competitive scoring is gated on **PAR calibration** — the per-scenario baseline every score is measured against — and PAR is deliberately unpublished until the engine implements each scenario's card abilities faithfully. Publishing baselines against a partly-implemented engine would bake wrong numbers into permanently version-pinned scores, so the gate stays closed on purpose.
>
> Practically: you can play these matches today, but a finished run will not yet post to the board. When calibration publishes, the boards open. The pool-construction problem above is unchanged either way — it comes from the cards, not the scoring.

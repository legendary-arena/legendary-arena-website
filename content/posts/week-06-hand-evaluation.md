Every turn presents the same question: what do you do with what you have? Your hand gives you a pool of recruit points and attack. The recruitment row offers cards you could add to your deck. The board presents threats you could fight. The decision feels open-ended, and open-ended decisions are where mistakes hide.

The keep/buy/skip framework gives you a fast, repeatable way to evaluate every recruitment opportunity — and the discipline to say no when saying no is the best move.

## The framework

Every card available for recruitment falls into one of three categories:

### Keep — cards that directly serve your strategy

A "keep" card fits your [strategy sentence](/posts/week-01-deck-checklist/), fills a role your deck needs, and sits at the right point on your [resource curve](/posts/week-02-resource-curve/). When you see a keep card and have the recruit points, buy it without hesitation.

The test is simple: does this card make your deck do its thing better? If your strategy is "draw-heavy engine that scales into a big finish turn," a card that draws two and adds one attack is a keep. A card that provides raw recruit with no synergy is not — even if it is technically powerful.

### Buy — cards that are good enough to improve your deck, but not perfect

A "buy" card is strong on its own but does not directly serve your primary strategy. It fills a gap, provides utility, or offers general value. Buy cards are worth recruiting when no keep card is available and your recruit points would otherwise go to waste.

The danger of buy cards is accumulation. One buy card is fine. Five buy cards and your deck has no identity. If you find yourself recruiting buy cards three turns in a row, your strategy sentence is either wrong or your build is drifting.

### Skip — cards you should not recruit even though you can afford them

Skip is the hardest category because it requires discipline. A skip card is one that does not serve your strategy, does not fill a needed role, and would dilute your deck even though it looks appealing.

The most common skip trap: a high-cost, high-impact card that does something impressive but unrelated to your game plan. It is powerful in a vacuum, but in your deck it is a brick that shows up at the wrong time and displaces a draw of something you actually need.

## Evaluating your hand

Before you look at the recruitment row, evaluate what your hand can do this turn:

1. **Total recruit points.** What is your spending budget?
2. **Total attack.** What threats can you address?
3. **Surplus or deficit.** Are you over-funded for recruiting (high recruit, low attack) or over-funded for fighting (high attack, low recruit)? This shapes your priority for the turn.

If you have surplus recruit points, look for a keep card. If no keep card is available, consider a buy card or bank the surplus turn toward thinning instead. If you have surplus attack, commit it to the highest-priority threat and do not recruit unless a keep card is too good to pass.

## Opportunity cost

Every card you recruit enters your deck and dilutes future draws. A 35-card deck draws each card approximately once every seven turns. A 40-card deck draws each card once every eight turns. That difference means your best cards show up less often, your engine takes longer to assemble, and your late-game turns are weaker.

The opportunity cost of buying a "pretty good" card is not its recruit price — it is every future turn where you draw it instead of something better. Skip is often the highest-value play.

## Reading the recruitment row

Scan the row in this order:

1. **Is there a keep card I can afford?** Buy it.
2. **Is there a keep card I cannot afford?** Plan for it next turn (if the row is stable) or accept it may leave.
3. **Is there a buy card that fills a genuine gap?** Recruit it — but only if you have identified the gap.
4. **Is everything a skip?** Congratulations. Save your recruit points for thinning, or accept a "dead" recruit turn. A dead recruit turn with a lean deck is better than a live recruit turn that bloats it.

## When "good cards" are bad buys

A card can be objectively powerful and subjectively wrong for your deck. Two examples:

**The expensive finisher you cannot support.** An eight-cost card that deals massive damage is useless if your economy never generates eight recruit in one turn. By the time you can afford it, the game may already be decided.

**The efficient card that fights your synergies.** A three-cost card that provides two recruit is efficient, but if your deck triggers bonuses from attack cards and this card provides recruit instead, it breaks your chains. Efficiency without synergy is a trap.

## Try this

Next game, before each recruitment decision, say the category out loud: keep, buy, or skip. If you say "buy" more than twice in a session, review whether those purchases actually improved your deck or just gave you something to do with leftover recruit points.

You are behind. The Scheme is advancing faster than you can respond. Villains are accumulating. Your deck is not producing enough output to match the Scenario's tempo. The instinct is to do more — recruit harder, attack more aggressively, take risks. That instinct accelerates the loss.

The recovery loop is a structured process that replaces panic with diagnosis and targeted action. It does not guarantee a comeback, but it maximizes the probability of one.

## Step 1: Stop the bleeding

Identify the single largest source of position loss. Not the most dramatic threat — the one that is costing you the most per turn. A Villain with a persistent penalty that fires every round. A Scheme twist threshold you are about to cross. A board state that compounds each turn.

Address that source. Redirect your resources from offense to containment. This may mean skipping a strong recruit to buy a defensive card, or splitting attack away from the highest-value target to address the one that is eroding your position fastest.

You are not trying to win yet. You are trying to stop losing.

## Step 2: Diagnose

Once the immediate bleeding stops, diagnose why you fell behind. The answer determines your recovery strategy:

**Deck problem:** Your build does not match the matchup. Your curve is wrong, your synergies are not firing, or your engine never assembled. The fix is targeted recruitment to patch the structural gap.

**Sequencing problem:** Your deck is fine but you played it poorly — wrong attack targets, missed synergy triggers, bad turn order. The fix is tighter execution with the cards you already have.

**Variance problem:** Your deck and play were both solid but you drew poorly — critical cards at the bottom, dead hands at critical moments. The fix is patience. Variance corrects over time if your deck is built correctly.

**Matchup problem:** The Scenario specifically counters your strategy. The fix is adaptation — shift your flex slots and accept that you are playing from a disadvantage.

Each diagnosis leads to a different recovery plan. Treating a variance problem like a deck problem (panic-recruiting new cards) makes things worse. Treating a deck problem like a variance problem (waiting for better draws) wastes turns.

## Step 3: Stabilize

Apply the [stabilization principles](/posts/week-19-stabilization/): match the Scenario's tempo. You do not need to get ahead — you need to stop falling further behind. The stabilization threshold is the point where your output per turn matches the Scenario's escalation per turn.

Signs you have stabilized:
- The board state is not getting worse each turn
- Your hand quality is consistent (no more dead-draw turns)
- You can make meaningful decisions each turn instead of just reacting

## Step 4: Build one advantage at a time

After stabilization, do not try to catch up all at once. Identify one dimension where you can gain a small advantage — one stronger recruit, one more efficient attack allocation, one synergy trigger that fires. Stack these small advantages over multiple turns.

The comeback is not a single explosive turn. It is a sequence of turns where you gain slightly more than you lose until the cumulative advantage reaches a tipping point. This is slower than a leading player's position but more reliable than a desperate gamble.

## When the recovery is not available

Some games are unrecoverable. If your diagnosis reveals a fundamental matchup problem that no amount of adjustment can fix, and the Scenario is past the point where stabilization is possible, the game is lost. Recognize it, play out the remaining turns for practice value, and take the lessons into the next round.

Knowing when to accept a loss is part of competitive play. A player who wastes mental energy on unwinnable games arrives at the next round fatigued. A player who accepts the loss and resets arrives fresh.

## Try this

Next time you fall behind, run through the four steps explicitly: stop the bleeding (what is the biggest drain?), diagnose (why am I behind?), stabilize (match the tempo), build (one advantage at a time). Track whether the structured approach produces a better outcome than the instinctive "fight harder" response.
